<u><b>Q1(i): Justify the company's decision to change its strategy.</b></u>
**The company decides to stop selling conventional without-profits immediate annuities**
- The annuity business may be unprofitable.
- The legislation has removed restrictions on the type of benefits that can be taken on retirement;
- so new business sales of immediate annuities is expected to fall;
- as the increased flexibility mean fewer policyholders are purchasing annuities and choosing the lump sum option instead.
- The company may be unsure on how to price the product;
- and it may be stopping sales until it is clearer what the demand for immediate annuities would be.
- The company may wish to avoid anti-selection;
- as following the legislation change, those in good health are more likely to purchase immediate annuities.
- Immediate annuities may be considered poor value for money;
- due to low interest rates and improvements in longevity.
- The company may no longer find it economical to sell annuity business due low new business sales leading to rising per policy expenses and inability to cover fixed expenses.

**The company decides to sell lifetime mortgage equity release products and conventional without-profits endowment assurances**
- The company may believe these products can replace the lost profits from not selling immediate annuities. 
- The company may believe these products are economical to sell and are able to cover fixed expenses. 
- The company may decide to sell two products to increase their product range;
- and gain diversification which may reduce capital requirements.
- The company may be reacting to input from distributors. 

**The company decides to sell equity release product**
- The demand for equity release products may be high in the UK;
- due to aging population;
- due to strong house inflation;
- due to reduction in financial support offered by pensions;
- The company may have determined that many individuals have built up equity in their homes;
- or may believe that those retiring would use the pension benefits to invest in the property market; thus increasing potential.
- The company may believe it may use the income from Product B to support the capital requirements of Product A.

**The company decides to sell single premium endowment assurances**
- The company may believe the product would be competitive given the high guarantees. 
- Tranches of endowment assurances could be an alternative to an annuity.
- The company would need capital to fund the equity release product and it may believe the single premium from this product will provide such capital.
- The company may believe that this is a niche product and as such sales volumes would be high resulting in high profits.

<u><b>Q1(ii): Assess the extent to which longevity or mortality is a key risk for Product A and B.</b></u>
**Product A**
- There is longevity risk as if people life longer than expected, there is a risk that the loan repayable exceeds the house value;
- as the loan continues to accumulate interest over the longer period.
- There is also a higher risk of house price crash over the longer period.
- The company may have considered Product A loan repayments as assets to be a match for liabilities under Product B.
- Alternatively, once Product A portfolio has matured enough, the loans provided to new policyholders of Product A could be considered well matched by the loan repayments from existing policyholders.
- The older the policyholder, the higher the volatility of expected to actual time to repayment;
- resulting in higher risk.
- There is mortality risk as if there are more deaths than expected, it leads to loss of future profits due to lost interest from loan.
- If the interest rate charged to the policyholder is higher than the discount rate used to value the loan;
- then a surplus results for each year the policy is inforce;
- and this surplus will be larger if more policyholders survive.
- This is assuming that the interest rate set in pricing the product includes a profit margin.

**Product B**
- There is no longevity risk as the sum assured is payable on maturity or earlier death.
- There is mortality risk as if there are more deaths than expected, payments are made earlier than expected, reducing profits.
- However, mortality risk is not a significant risk for Product B.

<u><b>Q1(iii): Describe the considerations in relation to setting the early exit penalites.</b></u>
**For both products**
- Considerations should be given to TCF requirements.
- There should be no unreasonable barriers to exit.
- The penalties should not be unfair to both continuing and existing policyholders. 
- The penalties should be easy to calculate.
- The penalties should be documented, explained, and understood by the policyholder at outset. 
- The penalties should cover the costs associated with the early redemption, e.g. sending payment and updating systems.
- The company should only reflect in the penalties the loss of future profits that were expected to be made;
- which would be small for Product B policies that are close to maturity.
- The company may reduce from the penalties the future expected maintenance expenses which would no longer be incurred.
- The company should ensure the surrender value is not too low compared to premiums paid, particularly at early durations.
- The company should smooth the surrender value by duration.
- The company should consider the penalties imposed by competitors;
- as it wants its products to be attractive, so it will want to avoid setting penalties that are out of line with competitors.
- The company should not change the terms of the penalty without valid reason.
- The company should not change the penalties unless dictated by economic conditions.

**For Product A**
- There is a risk the policyholder repays the loan early when market interest rates are low and better rates can be obtained elsewhere. 
- The penalty should reflect the resultant loss to the company, based on the difference between the future interest it would have received on the loan and the income it would receive on investing the repayment.

**Product B**
- There is a risk that policyholders surrender if they think they can get a better return by investing in other savings products;
- as higher interest rates will lead to lower values for the assets backing Product B.
- In particular, the company may incur a loss if it has used Product A as an asset to match Product B's liabilities. 
- Any such loss should be reflected in the penalty.
- Customers may surrender early for other reasons. In this case. provided borrowing rates at the time are lower than the guaranteed rate of return, no penalty should apply.
- The penalty could be reduced by future mortality costs under Product B, as there will be no longer be required.
- However, these may not be significant and so may be ignored.

<u><b>Q1(v): Describe the factors the company would take into considerations in the development of the two products.</b></u>
**Profitability**
- The company will need to ensure the products are profitable. 
- The company would want to ensure the products meet the company's profit criteria;
- which may be in the net present value of profits at a risk discount rate expressed as a percentage of sales volume;
- or internal rate of return;
- or discounted payback period.
- To assess profitability, the company may make best estimate assumptions about future experience in their pricing model;
- such as mortality accounting for the level of underwriting and the defined target market;
- as well as new business volume, new business mix, investment return, expenses, inflation, surrenders, etc.
- The company does not have any experience for this product;
- so it may seek external advice or data, such as from reinsurers;
- and add higher margins due to uncertainty when pricing the product.
- The company may consider how the products appear in the published accounts to assess the impact on embedded value (e.g. investigate level of profitability under reporting assumptions).
- Pricing assumptions for Product A include long-term care transfer rates, future house price growth, and early redemptions.
- Pricing assumptions for Product B include the investment return.

**Sensitivity of profit**
- The company will need consider the risks and carry out stress tests to determine the sensitivities; 
- which will help determine the appropriate risk strategies. 
- For Product A: 
- The key risks are house price inflation, long-term care transfer rates, changes in interest rates, and liquidity. 
- For Product B: 
- The product offers a guaranteed rate of return so is exposed to risk of changes in investment returns.
- The company should consider sensitivity to surrender rates and how this is reduced by any early exit penalties. 
- The company is exposed to the risk that new business volumes are higher than assumed when pricing;
- leading to capital strain and strain on administration.
- However, the risk of higher sales than expected should be limited for Product B as not all maturity terms are available;
- and as such the company may limit potential business volumes for Product A.
- There is also the risk that new business volumes are lower than expected;
- increasing the risk that initial expenses cannot be recouped. 
- The company is exposed to operational risks related to pricing and administration as the products are complex. 
- If the company backs Product B's liabilities with corporate bonds, it is exposed to credit risk.

**Marketability / Competitiveness**
- The company will need to ensure the product is attractive to customers;
- particularly in comparison to similar products offered by competitors. 
- It needs to identify key competitors in the market and investigate the features/price of their products. 
- Extensive market research may be needed.
- For Product A: 
- The marketability will depend on the loan amount available, the loan interest, and early exit charges. 
- The marketability will depend on State pension and long-term care provisions.
- It will depend on how this product fits with other post-retirement income the customer may receive. 
- For Product B: 
- The marketability will depend on the premium rates, the guarantees, and early exit charges. 
- The marketability will depend on the state of the economy, e.g. current interest rates and how they compare to the guarantee. 
- The marketability will depend on the number of different maturity terms on offer; and the extent this may change. 

**Suitability to meet customer needs for defined target market**
- The company will need to ensure the product meets the customer needs. 
- The needs met by the two products may be quite different, will want to make sure it is able to identify these needs and design the respective products to meet them.
- For Product A:
- The product is most suited to customers who are asset rich but income poor;
- i.e. those who own their homes but have limited pension provision. 
- The loan is normally repaid after death means this cost is met by the policyholder's estate, which reduces payments to dependents and so may not meet customer needs. 
- For Product B:
- The product may have a desirable guarantee in the economic climate of low interest rates. 
- If the customer purchases the product for the pension income in retirement, they may be disappointed if their funds run out, i.e. the product does not provide sufficient income for rest of lifetime;
- as the product does not offer protection over the risk of living longer than expected.

**Distribution and remuneration**
- The company will need to choose the distribution method. 
- The products are complex and will need explanation or advice to help customers choose the right product.
- The company may choose independent advisers over direct marketing. 
- The company will then need to decide how to remunerate the advisers (e.g. upfront payment). 
- The company needs to ensure this cost is allowed for in its premium/charges. 
- However, it may instead be paid by the customer directly as adviser fee.
- For Product A: 
- The customers are likely older so financial advisers who offer face to face consultancy may be the most appropriate channel.
- For Product B:
- Product B has the added complexity of customers needing to choose the particular maturity dates, which leans to financial advisers being the most appropriate channel.

**Financing/capital requirements**
- The company will need to consider financing and ensure it has sufficient capital to support the products.
- It should look at how they are treated under current solvency capital requirement regulations.
- If it has insufficient capital, the company will need to raise additional capital and consider the costs in doing so.
- For Product A:
- The product requires a large amount of capital upfront, in order to provide the loan to the policyholder.
- There may be other additional costs associated with this product, e.g. expenses related to the valuation of the house. 
- The reserves may also be high due to the no negative equity guarantee. 
- The capital requirements for Product A would be very high due to the high risks underlying equity release products.
- For Product B:
- The product will be expected to have low capital requirements as it is a single premium savings product. 
- The company should consider whether the product meets regulatory requirements that allow the company to hold lower reserves (e.g. the matching adjustment under Solvency II).
- This may involve restructuring the asset portfolio.  
- If the company is relying on the premiums from Product B to fund the upfront payment of Product A, then it will be dependent on the sales from Product B. 
- Even if sales were sufficient, there may be a mismatch between Product A and Product B due to the different maturity terms of Product B that customers choose;
- which may lead reinvestment risk or interest rate risk.
- The company could decide to convert some of its existing assets (which are currently backing the immediate annuities) into cash in order to fund Product A;
- but the company may then lose its eligibility to use the matching adjustment under Solvency II;

**Onerousness of any options or guarantees**
- The company needs to consider the onerousness/cost of the guarantees provided.  
- For Product A:
- This product offers a guaranteed loan rate to the customer; 
- and there is a risk if the company borrows upfront cash, this interest rate turns out higher than the guaranteed loan rate.
- The company may look to hedge this interest rate risk.
- There is also no negative equity guarantee (NNEG) on this product so the company is exposed to the risk that house values fall below the loan accumulated with interest.
- For Product B:
- The guaranteed return exposes the company to the risk of not being able to match this rate via its return on assets.
- Product A may be seen as a good match as the loan repayments under Product A could be used to provide the payments under product B. This introduces the risk that sales of Product A are not sufficient to provide this match.
- Product A’s early repayment option will introduce further uncertainty and hence risks for the company. 

**Extent of cross-subsidies** 
- The company needs to consider the extent of any cross-subsidies between the two products;
- as any such subsidy will introduce new business mix risk.
- The overall profit target may only be met by both products combined;
- or by larger products subsidising smaller ones.

**Company reputation and treating customers fairly**
- The company needs to ensure that it treats its customers fairly; 
- and that it maintains is reputation.
- The company could consider whether the products meet environmental, social and governance (ESG) standards. 
- The company would be keen to avoid any potential mis-selling issues.
- These are complex products. The company can help to ensure that customers are treated fairly by ensuring that they are fully aware of product characteristics before purchasing.
- For Product A:

- The company will want to ensure that it is seen as fair to those customers who decide to repay the loan early.
- The company will need to decide how to deal with others who may be living in the house when a policyholder dies. 
- It will also need to consider how it manages and monitors the upkeep of the house.
- For Policy B:
- The company will want to make sure that the customer is aware, by purchasing this product, that they still hold the risk of living longer than their funds can provide for.

**Administration systems / service standards**
- The company will have to consider the service standards and its administration / IT / valuation systems.
- As these are new products, there will likely be substantial set-up costs (e.g. development of systems).

<u><b>Q1(vi): Describe how the company should manage liquidity risk.</b></u>
**Liquidity controls**
- The company should match cash outflows with cash inflows.
 - Potential mismatches between cash outflows of loans to customers under Product A and inflows of single premiums under Product B also be identified. 
- The company should ensure it has sufficient controls in place;
- i.e. to identify timing and amount of uncertain claim amounts, for example non-maturity claims under Product B.
- The company should consider liquidity in the company's risk policy;
- and set out requirements for monitoring, measuring, reporting and limiting this liquidity risk.
- The company should consider liquidity within its risk-based capital framework;
- e.g. using scenario analysis to assess potential liquidity issues in the future.
- The company should identify circumstances in which customers can exercise options, e.g. surrendering under Product B when interest rates are high.
- The company should ensure it holds an adequate amount of liquid assets to allow for unexpected liquidity requirements, e.g. cash holdings. 
- The company could consider limiting new business volumes.
- The company could consider using derivatives to match its liabilities more closely.
- The company could consider entering into a counterparty arrangement which provides short-term cash in an emergency, e.g. agreeing lines of credit. 
- The company should manage the volatility of income from the equity release assets.
