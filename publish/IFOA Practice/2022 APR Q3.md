<u><b>Q3(ii): Describe the risks to Company A arising from this arrangement.</b></u>
**Insurance risk**
- There is expense risk, where selling expenses are higher than expected.
- There is new business volume risk if Company A sells significantly higher / lower volumes of the income protection product (with the new rider) than expected.
- There is new business mix risk if Company A uses any cross-subsidies in its pricing.
- Company A may have limited experience at negotiating terms, so the product with the rider may turn out to be loss-making. 

**Credit / counterparty risk**
- There is counterparty risk with Company B; 
- as Company A is exposed to Company B becoming insolvent;
- and Company A might feel obliged to compensate the policyholders.
- Additional credit risk arises from the agreement of payments between Company A and Company B..

**Liquidity risk**
- There is liquidity risk for Company A; 
- e.g. if there is a delay in Company B paying over its share of a claim.

**Operational risk**
- There is operational risk;
- e.g. mis-selling of the rider;
- as it may not be appropriate for some policyholders.
- Additional operational risk arises for Company A from the sharing of control of the product;
- as well as around the practicalities in apportioning the premium payments. 
- There is reputational risk;
- e.g. adverse publicity for Company B could have a negative impact on Company A;
- which could negatively impact new business sales for Company A;
- as well as persistency on its other products.
- There is a risk of the regulator making an adverse unexpected change which prohibits the selling of the rider. 
- There is legal risk; 
- around the agreement between the two companies.

<u><b>Q3(vi): Discuss the relative merits of using a marginal basis for pricing this product.</b></u>
**Expenses** 
- This would involve using marginal expenses;
- which do not consider overhead expenses as these are assumed to be covered by other products.
- This will result in the new product covering lower expenses than those priced on a standard pricing basis.
- This may result in a lower price charged;
- or a higher per policy profit recognised. 
- Pricing using marginal expenses may result in a more competitive market position and thus higher volumes.
- However, other products will be covering the overheads 
- and this might not be sustainable in the long term, 
- as the equity release business volumes increase.

**Capital**
- Consider using a marginal basis when allowing for the cost of capital.
- This will result in the new product recognising all of the diversification benefits.
- This may result in more competitive pricing;
- or higher profits being made. 
- Marginal costing may not be permitted on a valuation basis, so pricing and valuation will be inconsistent.
- As equity release products are capital intensive, there will be huge benefits from recognising the diversification potential.
- An alternative is to price using a basis under which the additional diversification benefits are shared across all relevant products.

<u><b>Q3(vii): Discuss the key considerations the company would make when deciding how to implement this metric.</b></u>
**Objective** 
- The company needs be clear on the reason why it has chosen to add such a metric;
- e.g. to be able to rank projects based on capital efficiency. 
- Company A would consider whether the RoC meets the objective.
- It would consider whether the components of the metric can be applied consistently. 
- This metric might be particularly useful if Company A has limited capital.
- Company A would probably want to use the metric alongside other metrics when making a decision.
- It needs to consider how to deal with any conflicting results.
- For example, if one project shows a higher RoC but a lower net present value than another one, how should Company A decide on which of these to choose?
- To be able to compare projects, it will be important to be consistent in its calculation
- e.g. regarding assumptions used in deriving profit.

**Definition**
- Company A needs to define the metric, i.e.: RoC = profit measure / capital invested. 
- Decide whether the capital will include any additional diversification benefits. 
- Decide which expenses are deducted from the profit measure, e.g. development costs,
- and the extent to which any overheads are included. 
- Company A needs to decide how to define the profit measure.
- e.g. will Company A use its statutory accounting profit, e.g. under IFRS, or its embedded value profit?
- It needs to decide whether the profit measure will be stated gross or net of tax. 

**Practicalities**
- Company A would consider how easy the metric is to calculate. 
- It would consider whether it could calculate the metric at all times.
- Company A should consider testing the appropriateness of the metric
- for example by testing it on existing successful projects.
- If it was decided not to use the RoC metric, Company A may need to decide on an alternative metric.
