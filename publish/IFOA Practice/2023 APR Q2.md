<u><b>Q2(i): Explain why a company may want to hold assets that are in excess of its liabilities.</b></u>
**Risk capital**
- Life insurance companies  are exposed to many risks.
- The company would hold need to risk capital;
- to provide a buffer against adverse day-to-day movements in the value of assets and liabilities.

**Regulatory capital**
- The regulator likely requires additional capital to be held, e.g. SCR in Solvency II.
- The company might hold higher capital to avoid any regulatory capital add-on being required.
- If the company's available capital falls below the regulatory required capital, the regulator may impose sanctions;
- so it will want to hold a buffer to reduce the risk of such events.
- It might set the lower end of its preferred capital range to be some way above the regulatory required capital level so that, if the level falls below the preferred range, there is time to take action before failing to meet regulatory requirements.
- The size of the gap between the regulatory required capital and the lower end of the preferred capital range will depend on the company’s risk appetite.
- The company may be targeting a capital level that is comparable with competitors.
- The company might be expecting a regulatory change.

**Rating agency capital**
- The company may be targeting a particular credit rating with rating agencies 
- allowing it to borrow at a cheaper rate, if required.

**Working capital** 
- The company would need to hold working capital.
- This could allow it to:
	- write its target volume of new business 
	- support the business / meet strategic objectives
	- carry out business developments such as launch a new product / build a new IT system
	- increase investment freedom, to achieve higher expected returns
	- acquire other companies or blocks of business
	- smooth bonuses on with-profits business.

<u><b>Q2(ii): Discuss the company's response to holding significantly higher available capital than target.</b></u>
**No action**
- The company might not take action
- For example, there might have been an error in the calculation which needs to be checked.
- Investigations might show that the position is likely to be temporary 
- and so taking action to reduce the available capital would not be appropriate. 
- Alternatively, there might already be a plan in place to use the excess capital.

**Return to owners** 
- The company might decide to reduce the available capital by returning it to the shareholders.
- This could be done through increased dividends;
- or a share buyback.
- The company might decide to repay any debt held. 
- If the excess capital is held in a with-profits fund, the company might decide to distribute it to the with-profits policyholders via higher bonuses.
- It is also necessary to consider whether the estate is excessive.
- if so, the company will want to treat its with-profits customers fairly;
- by distributing the estate, e.g. by reducing / removing charges to asset shares.

**Increase risk** 
- The company could take on greater investment risk;
- e.g. by taking a less matched position;
- or by reducing the amount of derivatives held (or any other sensible example). 
- The company could reduce its use of reinsurance / recapture reinsured business.
- If reinsurance is reduced, the resultant greater mortality risk will increase required capital;
- although there is some offset to this through lower counterparty risk.
- The aim would be for the available capital to now fall within the target range;
- although the company would also expect increased future profits;
- which might increase available capital.

**Support the business / take opportunities** 
- The company could use the capital to increase future profits for shareholders.
- It could use the capital to: 
	- write higher volumes of new business
	- reduce premiums in order to increase market share 
	- invest in infrastructure
	- buy another company / block of business.
- Investing in infrastructure could increase future profits through lower expenses.

**Stochastic modelling** 
**Advantages include:** 
- provides important insight into how the economic balance sheet is affected across a range of scenarios
- better enables companies to model complex interactions / dynamic assumptions
- allows testing of management actions under certain scenarios 
- helps the company prepare plans for changing circumstances 
- models financial guarantees and options more appropriately.

**Disadvantages include:**
- more complex modelling 
- takes more time / cost / expertise to develop
- more care needs to be taken when calibrating the model
- setting assumptions
- and interpreting the output.

**Stress tests**
**Advantages include: ·**
- ‘time zero’ stress tests are a simpler way of assessing the current risk position
- easier to explain to non-technical decision-makers, such as the board
- can use reverse stress testing to identify ‘killer scenarios’ 
- that could make the company insolvent
- thus focussing management effort on actions that could be taken to mitigate the likelihood of such scenarios.
