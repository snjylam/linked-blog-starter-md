<u><b>Q4(i): Describe the main features of an ‘I minus E’ taxation approach.</u></b>
- Tax is payable on the excess of I over E, where: 
	- I typically comprises total investment earnings;
	- possibly excluding equity dividends;
	- and possibly also excluding equity / property unrealised gains.
	- E typically comprises expenses, possibly with acquisition costs being spread over a defined period;
	- E might also include the income component of general annuities.
	- and possibly also including an amount of XSE carried forward from the previous tax year.
- This ‘I minus E’ amount might then be split into shareholder profit and policyholder profit components;
- with a different tax rate being applied to each component.
- A minimum profits test might then be applied to a proprietary company, so that the taxable amount is the higher of ‘I minus E’ and trading profit.
- If the minimum profits test bites, the unrelieved amount of E can be carried forward into the next tax year as XSE.
- The minimum profits test ensures that the insurance company pays at least the same amount of corporation tax as would otherwise be expected.
- Policyholders might have to pay additional tax, depending on the regime;
- e.g. higher-rate policyholders might have to pay extra tax at the excess of their marginal tax rate over the basic rate of income tax.

<u><b>Q4(ii): Suggest reasons for the government’s proposal to replace 'I minus E' with a tax on pre-tax trading profit.</u></b>
**Issues relating to protection business**
- It may be too penal and disincentivise taking out protection products.
- Alternatively, it may be too generous and be disincentivising taking out other products.
- It may not be providing the expected tax revenue.
- Protection business typically has low I and high E;
- and this E could be being offset against XSI in insurance companies that also sell savings business.
- The proposal might encourage more new protection specialist entrants into the market.

**Practicalities**
- The current tax basis may be too complicated;
- e.g. difficulties in determining effective tax rate (split between policyholder and shareholder).
- It may be out of line with other countries;
- which could cause issues with financial equivalence between territories.
- There may be loopholes in the current tax approach that are being utilised.
- New products may have been designed which are not well catered for under the current tax regime;
- i.e. the definition of which products are I-E may be unclear.

<u><b>Q4(iii): Discuss the impact of the government’s proposal on life insurance companies that are subject to taxation in this country.</u></b>
**General**
- The level of impact depends on:
	- whether the change is applied retrospectively to include existing protection business;
	- if it is then this could materially impact the company’s profits;
	- whether company was XSI or XSE prior to change;
	- whether firm is a proprietary or mutual;
	- the overall mix of business / volume of protection business.

**Impact on the business sold**
- If the change only applies to new business, then the effect may be more limited.
- The company may be able to alter its new business product offering to make it more tax-efficient under the new regime;
- and so pricing assumptions may have to change;
- but altering the product offering to make it more tax-efficient may involve passing some of the cost onto the policyholders;
- so insurers would need to consider competitive pressures;
- and meeting policyholder needs.
- Some firms may exit the protection market;
- but the change might encourage new protection specialist life insurance companies to enter into the market;
- and more protection specialists entering the market could increase price competition.
- Tax is applied to profits on the business rather than I-E and so will factor in the value of income/outgo from premiums and claims.
- Rather than using a mixture of the corporation and policyholder tax rates;
- the tax calculations for protection business will now only use the corporation tax rate.
- Any changes to the product may impact new business volumes.
- Reinsurance deals might need to be revisited;
- if they are no longer tax-efficient / could be more beneficial.
- Other strategies to reduce the tax bill might be investigated.
- There could be changes to the savings market as well as the protection market.

**Practicalities**
- There will be an additional cost to the insurer in adopting to the new regime:
	- e.g. systems updates 
	- e.g. assumption changes
	- e.g. training.
- There may also be higher ongoing costs:
	- e.g. due to the need to provide additional details / calculate additional information under the new regime
	- e.g. due to the need to maintain an additional tax calculation if this only applies to new business.
- There will be an additional cost of staff training, when implementing the change.  

<u><b>Q4(iv): Discuss the suggestion to remove the tax charge on surrender because it is unfair to policyholders who surrender due to poor service or unforeseen circumstances.</u></b>
**Fairness**
- Taking a tax charge on surrender seems fair, as the charge is normally applied to the excess of the benefit received over premiums paid, which reflects the ‘profit’ to the policyholder.
- Not taking a tax charge on surrender is likely to be seen as unfair compared with those who remain in-force to maturity or who die.
- Taxation on surrender can be viewed as a penalty in that the tax occurs earlier in the life of the policy;
- rather than at maturity, accelerating the tax paid by the policyholder;
- so, in some ways this could be seen as unfair to policyholders who feel they need to surrender. 
- It is also possible it is beneficial as the policyholder may become a higher rate tax policyholder in future, so the total tax bill over the policyholder’s life could be reduced 
- although this is less likely. 
- However, if tax is not paid on surrender, this effectively lowers the total tax bill for the policyholder ;
- and so will not be consistent with the tax paid by those who remain in force. 
- The current approach seems fair in that:
	- it is expected that less tax would be payable on surrender than on the full maturity value;
	- it is consistent with how gains on other investments would be taxed, if sold before maturity.

**Anti-selection**
- The change is likely to encourage policyholders to surrender, to benefit from lower tax;
- particularly shortly before maturity;
- depending on surrender values (and penalties) compared with maturity values.

**Surrender rates**
- The level of policies surrendering will increase;
- and there may be a large wave of surrenders if this is introduced.
- Insurance companies will bear the surrender costs and lower levels of business;
- and company profits may fall.
- Partial surrenders, paid up policies, and alterations may become less viable if only surrenders attract zero taxation rather than other options. 
- The additional costs arising for insurers in relation to higher surrenders might be passed on to the policyholders that remain in-force.

**Practicalities**
- Surrender value quotations/illustrations will need be amended.
- System changes may be required (e.g. for some jurisdictions company pays tax on behalf of policyholder) 

**Government perspective**
- Lower tax paid to the government might result in a need to take more tax in other ways / increase tax rates.
- Encouraging policyholders to surrender might go directly against government objectives;
- e.g. in ensuring that policyholders build up adequate savings for retirement.
- Companies may design policies in a way such that surrender close to maturity is the preference; 
- thereby avoiding the tax bill for policyholders altogether;
- so the government will need to legislate to avoid this.

<u><b>Q4(v): Discuss the possible advantages and disadvantages of the government’s proposal to merge regulators.</u></b>
**Advantages**
- There will be economies of scale from such an approach;
- reducing the overheads associated with regulation;
- and potentially reducing the cost to insurers/customers.
- The central bank will be better informed in situations where it may need to act as lender of last resort.
- Simplicity - Insurers will have a single clear point of contact.
- It will remove the possibility of conflicting regulation from the two regulatory bodies.
- It will remove duplication of regulation where the regulators cover the same area.
- May improve consistency between regulation of insurers and other financial industries.
- May be better suited to deal with large financial group companies that offer both insurance and other financial instruments. 
- Improved accountability and transparency - it is clear which regulator is responsible for an issue. 

**Disadvantages**
- There will be a cost in making the changes;
- and resource implications (short-term);
- and redundancies (long term).
- The changes may take a long time to fully implement.
- It is possible expertise will be lost in making the transition.
- There will be a period of disruption for both regulators and insurers.
- The focus given by having more than one regulator may be lost;
- (e.g.) loss of focus on customer outcomes;
- or loss of focus on insurer specific regulation, which could harm the insurance industry.
- Possible conflict of interests between being the central bank and the regulator;
	- e.g. may be less willing to let companies fail as it’s seen as the bank’s fault
	- e.g. may be more willing to loosen financial regulation in troubled times and this may not be beneficial to the economy. 
- May be a significant change in the new regulator’s “high risk areas” of focus causing further disruption for companies.
