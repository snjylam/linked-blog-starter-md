<u><b>Q3(i): Describe the impacts of combined stress on estate and required capital of with-profits fund, prior to management actions.</b></u>
**Own funds**
- The value of government bonds will increase due to the fall in interest rates.
- The value of corporate bond holdings will fall due to the widening of credit spreads.
- The value of equity and property holdings will fall.
- The value of overseas assets (relative to domestic liabilities) will rise due to currency movements.
- The overall impact on asset values (and hence on the estate / own funds) will depend heavily on the company’s asset mix.
- But a decrease in asset values is generally expected, leading to a reduction in own funds / free assets (the estate);
- as it is more likely because the estate is often invested more cautiously (cash / government bonds), but the assets backing the with-profits business are typically riskier and less likely to be fully protected.
- The asset share will be impacted according to the investment strategy of the with-profits fund. While the movement in asset share should broadly match the change in backing assets, the matching may not be exact.
- Any allowance for smoothing of bonuses may further impact own funds if it is assumed that the full market shock does not feed through immediately to the smoothed asset share.
- It is possible that asset shares fall below the level of guarantees already declared, which would create an additional strain on own funds.

**Cost of guarantees**
- The cost of guarantees on vesting annuities is likely to increase because:
    - The guarantee becomes more valuable relative to cash or the open market option;
    - The propensity of policyholders to take up the guarantee may rise;
    - The present value of the expected payout at maturity may increase (particularly if there are overseas holdings).
- The overall impact on guarantee costs will also depend on how “in-the-money” the guarantees currently are.
- The unitised with-profits fund may have a minimum guaranteed return, and the cost of this guarantee could also change.

**Risk Margin**
- Market risks are generally considered hedgeable and are not directly included in the risk margin.
- However, lower interest rates increase the risk margin because of the lower discount rate applied to future non-hedgeable risks.
- The risk margin may increase further due to any additional impacts from guarantees.
- Currency movements may increase the risk margin by raising the relative impact of non-hedgeable overseas risks.
- Overall, the risk margin is expected to increase.

**SCR (Solvency Capital Requirement)**
- The with-profits fund SCR is likely to be driven primarily by the interest rate down scenario;
- due to the increased cost of guarantees under stress;
- so a fall in interest rates is therefore likely to increase the SCR.
- The credit spread widening shock will reduce the credit risk component of the SCR (there is now a lower value of corporate bonds exposed to the stress).
- The equity and property shocks will also reduce the SCR because the base values are now lower (less to lose in the stress).
- The impact of exchange rate stresses will depend on the level of overseas assets and liabilities; 
- and any risk associated with overseas holdings are likely to increase.
- The overall impact on the SCR may be difficult to determine due to interactions between the various stresses and changes in diversification benefits with other risk modules.
- A reduction in SCR seems most likely overall, as most market risk stresses will apply to lower base values;
- and guarantee costs are likely already fully reflected in the existing SCR.

<u><b>Q3(ii): Discuss any management actions related to the WPF the company may want to incorporate into the scenario.</b></u>
**General comments**
- Action will depend on the actual impact on the with-profits fund 
- and whether the shock creates a temporary or permanent change.
- Policyholders’ reasonable expectations should be considered when determining the extent of any action.
- These expectations will depend on what has been communicated to policyholders in customer literature 
- in addition to what the company has done in the past 
- and what the industry is doing.
- The company may first consider making changes to its current practice around estate distribution
- such as removing any distribution away from policyholders who have guarantees heavily in the money
- or cancelling any historically assigned estate distribution.
- The company would need to decide how it would actually charge for the increased costs
- e.g. whether this is via a reduction in bonus rates 
- or via a charge to the asset share of the policies with the guarantee 
- or through a transfer out of the inherited estate
- or, if shareholders are sharing in the cost, via a transfer out of the shareholder fund
- and into the with-profits fund.
- The company must decide how any charges for the cost of the GAR should be divided between shareholder and policyholder.
- A realistic timescale of when any management action can be put in place must be considered.
- Any policyholder advocate (e.g. the With-Profits Actuary) will need to be consulted and any action agreed upon beforehand.
- All actions will be constrained by model limitations.

**Increase charges**
- The company may most likely increase or introduce charges to conventional with profits policyholders for the cost of guarantees.
- There will be less discretion for management to do this on unitised with profits contracts
- as and UWP policyholders may not expect charges to be increased
- but it may be possible to vary the existing charges on this book
- and given the market shock, policyholders will probably expect it. 
- For the UWP business, the costs may be paid for by the estate or via the shareholder fund. 
- The company may impose MVRs for surrendering policyholders. 
- This will protect the remaining UWP policyholders.

**Reduce bonuses**
- In the event of the shock the company may want to reduce any reversionary bonuses for the contracts. 
- In order to reduce the accumulation of guarantees.
- Or reduce final bonus rates.
- Or defer bonuses by switching from regular bonus to final bonus.
- This may only be practical for the conventional with profits contract.
- However past practice and policyholder documentation may limit the practical extent of this action
- as might the PPFM.

**Revise investment strategy**
- The company may want to de-risk balance sheet and investment strategy of the with profits fund
- e.g. by moving more towards government bonds.
- This would reduce the SCR and improve the capital position of the with profits fund
- and reduce the value of future guarantees.
- The company may want to hedge its GAR by investing in an interest rate swap, for example.
- Due to the increase in corporate bond spreads, the company would likely want to reduce credit risk
- to protect against future losses from a fall in asset values.
- Reducing credit risk could be carried out by changing the asset mix backing the asset share
- but this will depend on whether and what the policyholders expect, as they will have an expectation around the level of risk and return achieved on their policy.
- In contrast, there will be less restriction on the assets backing the estate. 
- Selling risky assets to buy safer assets would crystallise losses 
- and reduce future expected returns.

**Reinsurance** 
- The company could reduce the risk associated with the GAR
- via conventional insurance, removing all risks.
- or longevity swap to remove longevity risk.
- Reinsuring the with-profits policies may prove challenging.
- The reinsurance cover may be difficult to obtain and costly. 

**New business strategy**
- The company may want to allow for closure to new business, or to limit volumes
- or a reduced investment guarantee on unitised with profits contracts going forward 
- to reduce the build up of guarantees.
- For a mature with-profits fund this may have limited effect
- but should be allowed for if it is a likely outcome in the scenario.
- Actions considered may be constrained by model limitations.
- Implementing hedging after an economic shock will help reduce the risk of future market shocks.
- The cost associated with any hedging arrangement will need to be allowed for. 
- This cost will reflect the market price of the hedge under the stressed conditions. 
- Such post-shock purchasing may be too costly.

<u><b>Q3(iii): Discuss the factors the company should consider when deciding how to make an appropriate distribution of surplus.</b></u>
**Refunding charges**
- The company should first consider refunding charges made to asset share
- as these charges would have contributed to the excess surplus.
- When refunding charges, the company may choose to benefit those who have been charged the most.
- It should consider whether it is fair to distribute more to those with high guarantees.
- It should also consider whether charges should be refunded to policyholders that made a high return by investing when the markets were low.
- The company could give more to those who have suffered the most from the economic shock. 
- Alternatively, the company may base distribution on the policy duration.
- It should consider whether it is fair to favour those who have invested the longest when the surplus was created recently.

**Pace of distribution**
- The company will need to consider the pace of distribution 
- as well as the total amount distributed.
- It may consider holding surplus back 
- in case of future shocks
- but being mindful to avoid any tontine affect.
- The company would want to avoid the impression that it is overcompensating for excessive charges being taken after the economic shock
- as this could lead to an increase in withdrawals.
- If shareholders funded the impact from the shock, then the company needs to consider repaying them 
- and consider whether such a distribution would be fair to policyholders.
- It would not want to give the impression that preferential treatment was being provided. 

**Method of distribution**
- The refund could be carried out via additions to asset share
- which is simple
- and avoids increasing guaranteed benefits
- increases flexibility
- and may be seen as most equitable. 
- There may be pressures to increase reversionary bonuses
- which would reduce flexibility.
- Any increase in asset share would reduce the cost of any guarantees
- but if surrender values are based on asset share, this increase surrender values which may be undesirable
- also, those policyholders with high guarantees may see no change.
- If the company targets the asset share as the maturity payout, then any increase in reversionary bonuses means a decrease in terminal bonuses. 
- So if the company wants to increase payouts, then the asset share will need to increase in line with the extra reversionary bonuses declared.
- The refund could also be carried out via a special reversionary bonus to not increase future expectations.
- This would be easier to communicate to policyholders and could generate goodwill
- and policyholders should not expect any further special bonuses.

**Constraints and practicalities**
- In determining the surplus distribution it will be necessary to demonstrably treat customers fairly
- and to be equitable between different groups and generations of policyholders
- but this does not necessarily mean applying the same refund to all policyholders
- as it may favour those who have suffered particularly in terms of charges
- or have experienced low investment returns
- or those who have invested over the longest periods and therefore contributed the most.
- UWP and CWP are likely to have differing approaches to the surplus distribution.
- Any approach should also be in line with what has been carried out in the past 
- and what has been communicated
- e.g. via the PPFM.
- The company should also consider what other companies have done in similar circumstances. 
- The company would want to consult any policyholder advocate, such as the WPA.
- The Chief Actuary should be consulted 
- as well as any professional guidance, 
- and regulation.
- The company should consider any tax implications due to change in reported surplus in the accounts.
