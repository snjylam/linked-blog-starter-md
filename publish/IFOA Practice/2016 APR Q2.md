<u><b>Q2(i): Describe the three lines of defence in risk management.</b></u>
**Three lines of defence**
- First line is business operations;
- where a well established control environment is embedded in day-to-day operation.
- Second line is oversight function (e.g. standalone risk function);
- which is responsible for production, implementation, and monitoring of risk management processes.
- Third line is independent assurance providers (e.g. internal and external audit);
- which evaluate and challenge the company's risk management processes.

<u><b>Q2(ii): Describe the liquidity risk that may arise for the company.</b></u>
**Liquidity risk**
- Liquidity risk is the risk of mismatch in short-term cashflows.
- For this company, it is the risk of insufficient income from assets;
- or insufficient cash or short-term liquid assets holdings;
- to meet short-term annuity payments due to policyholders;
- and to meet short-term expenses.
- As a result, the company may be forced to sell assets at inopportune times;
- potentially making a loss.
- Liquid risk is also the risk where the company cannot realise assets quickly enough;
- for example, in the sale of property investments.

<u><b>Q2(iii): Describe the conduct risk that may arise for the company.</b></u>
**Conduct risk**
- This is the risk that the company behaves in a way that leads to poor customer outcomes.
- The risk may arise as a result of poor servicing, e.g. incorrect application of benefit increases; 
- or poor quality of communications. 
- The risk may arise due to information asymmetries;
- e.g. in relation to underwriting, if impaired life annuities are sold.
- The risk may arise due to failing to comply with regulations; e.g. in relation to the information given to customers when considering an annuity.
- The risk may arise due to poor control of distribution;
- e.g. if the advisers are not making it sufficiently clear to customers that they could be better off with an impaired life annuity.
- The risk may arise due to poor strategic decisions being made by the company;
- e.g. inappropriate product design.

<u><b>Q2(iv): Suggest methods by which credit risk arising from bonds can be controlled.</b></u>
**Credit risk controls**
- There should be limits on the level of exposure to each third party, i.e. amount invested in each bond.
- There should be an adequate level of diversification, i.e. invest across a wide range of bonds.
- There should be restrictions on investment based on credit ratings, e.g. only allow investment in bonds rated BBB or higher. 
- There should be frequent monitoring of credit risk exposures, including:
	- the counterparty exposure;
	- the likelihood of default; 
	- the potential recovery if a default occurs;
	- the period for which the exposure continues. 
- The company may use credit default swaps to hedge any risk of default from corporate bond issuers.
- There should be a good good governance framework and ensure there is a clear separation of accountabilities amongst the appropriate functions.

<u><b>Q2(v): Describe the nature of longevity risks including those associated with future mortality.</b></u>
**Longevity risk**
- The longevity risk is the risk that annuitants live longer than expected in pricing;
- which leads to lower profits than expected or even losses.
- If annuitants live longer than allowed for in the reserving assumptions, there may be pressure on capital position or solvency. 
- However, the additional solvency capital requirements should protect against improvements in longevity. 
- The longevity risk is significant as the company sells only annuities;
- so there is less opportunity to diversify it across products.
- There is related expense risk if annuitants live for longer than expected.
- There is related investment mismatch risk if actual longevity is different to the level that has been assumed in bond selection.
- There is related risk of not insufficient or unavailable assets of suitably long-term to match the liabilities, introducing reinvestment risk.

**Model / parameter / stochastic risk**
- The model risk is the risk that the model chosen to represent future longevity may not be appropriate or may contain errors. 
- Types of model risk include:
	- models that have been incorrectly specified;
	- models that have been correctly specified but incorrectly built and are used unknowingly;
	- models that have been correctly built and specified but are inappropriately used for a specific purpose;
	- models that have an inappropriate level of complexity (over-simplified or over-complex);
	- misinterpretation of results when an appropriate model has been used, e.g. applied to the wrong population.
- Even if the 'correct' model was chosen, there is parameter risk, which is the risk that parameters chosen do not adequately reflect the future experience of the annuitants.
- The model and parameter risks can be reduced by collecting more data. 
- If the company has a large portfolio of annuity business and has been writing it for many years, then it should be able to reduce these risks.
- Even if the 'correct' model and parameters were chosen, the outcome would be uncertain due to random fluctuations. 
- The random fluctuations would be lower if the portfolio size is larger.
- Uncertainties would still exist, particularly around very high ages due to limited data available. 

**Risks in determining base mortality** 
- The company is likely to use a standard mortality table as its base table (CMI '00' or '08).
- There is a risk that actual mortality experience differs from the standard table. 
- This may be due the target market differs by:
	- socio-economic group;
	- location. 
- The company's own mortality experience analysis may have contained errors;
- or may have been based on incorrect or insufficient data. 
- The company may not have allowed sufficiently for trends over time in the historic experience.

**Risk in modelling future mortality improvement**
- There is a risk that actual future mortality improvements differs from expected. 
- This risk is important for annuities given their long-term nature.
- There is a risk the company has not allowed for 'cohort effect', whereby improvements exhibit strong patterns by year of birth.
- If the company uses extrapolation approach to project past experience to model mortality improvements, there is a risk of making poor judgements;
- for example choosing how long a period of past trends to do the projection, and for how long.
- If the company uses explanatory approach to model cause specific mortality, there is a risk that the bio-medical process may not be fully understood;
- or be capable of being mathematically modelled. 
- The risk is exacerbated as company sells without-profits annuities, and may not update charges to reflect actual experience.
- The further mortality rates are projected, the more uncertain they are;
- due to difficulty in predicting medical advances;
- due to uncertainty in future changes in lifestyle (e.g. diet and exercise).
- There are complex interactions between a number of factors underlying future mortality improvements which make it difficult to estimate and model, e.g. smoking affects both lung cancer and heart disease.
- If impaired life annuities are sold, there is a risk that underwriting is inadequate and  longevity is not appropriately assessed. 
- There are also lower volumes of data, making it harder to predict future improvements for impaired lives.

<u><b>Q2(vi): Comment on the likely effectiveness of selling term assurance to reduce longevity risk.</b></u>
- The company would benefit from improvements in mortality rates by selling term assurance;
- as these reduce the number of deaths and therefore benefit payments. 
- This would provide a hedge against the mortality improvements on the annuity business.
- However, selling term assurance is unlikely to be a perfect hedge as the insured populations are likely to differ.
- For example, term assurance policyholders are typically of working age while annuitants are typically in retirement;
- and the geographical concentration of the populations may also differ;
- thus the rate of mortality improvements may differ between the two groups.
- Additionally, selling term assurance is unlikely to be a perfect hedge because it has a shorter duration than annuities. 
- Depending upon the size of the annuities book, it may take significant time to grow the size of the term assurance book to provide a viable hedge.

<u><b>Q2(vii): Outline other issues that could arise in relation to selling term assurance.</b></u>
- The company has no past experience in writing term assurance.

<u><b>Q2(viii): Describe two ways in which the company could transfer longevity risk.</b></u>
**Longevity swap**
- The longevity swap has two parties, the insurer and the counterparty;
- where the insurer pays a fixed series of payments to the counterparty;
- as agreed at outset of the swap;
- and in return the insurer receives a floating set of payments;
- that are linked to the actual annuity payments. 
- The collateral is normally required;
- and is calculated on a regular basis;
- and is the value of the swap at any given date, i.e. the PV of the floating leg less the PV of the fixed leg.
- The absolute value will be posted by one party to mitigate the risk of the other, with a positive value indicating that the  counterparty must post collateral to mitigate the risk run by the insurer;
- and a negative value indicating that the insurer would post the collateral. 
- To calculate the value of the swap, both parties need to agree on the discount rate;
- which is typically based on a swap curve.
- The floating payments may be based on generic population index rather than the actual annuity payments.
- If so, both parties would need to agree a mechanism for determining the assumed life expectancy in advance.

**Reinsurance**
- The insurer pays a premium;
- and the reinsurer pays an agreed proportion of the annuity payments.
- The reinsurance could be based on on 
	- original terms (based on insurer's premium rates); or 
	- risk premium (based on reinsurer's premium rates).
- The reinsurance could be done on:
	- quota share basis (the proportion is reinsured is fixed for each policy); or 
	- individual surplus basis (the proportion reinsured depends on a fixed retention limit, so can vary by policy). 
- The reinsurance is likely to be arranged via a treaty;
- which could cover the in-force business, future new business or both. 
- The total annuity payments above a certain level could be reinsured;
- - The total annuity payments above a certain level could be reinsured;
	- arranged as an aggregate excess of loss;
	- or a stop loss style agreement.

<u><b>Q2(ix): Compare the additional risks that arise under these approaches.</b></u>
**Counterparty risk** 
- There is counterparty risk;
- which is the risk that the counterparty failing to perform its contractual obligations; 
- or does not perform them in a timely manner.
- This may be due to the counterparty being in financial difficulties or insolvency. 
- For the longevity swap, default is most likely when floating payments are expected to be higher than fixed payments. 
- The risk is reduced if the counterparty has a high credit rating. 
- The risk is reduced by posting collateral, which introduces liquidity risk.
- The longevity swap may be traded through an exchange to manage counterparty risk.
- The reinsurance could include a deposit back agreement could be undertaken where the reinsurer deposits assets with the insurance company;
- or a clause which allows the insurance company to recapture the exposure if a certain event occurs, i.e. if the reinsurer suffers a downgrade in credit rating.

**Basis risk**
- For the longevity swap, there is basis risk if floating payments are based on generic mortality index (instead of actual payment).
- For the reinsurance, there is no basis risk as payments from the reinsurer depend on the actual annuity experience.

**Legal risk**
- For the longevity swap, it may be structured as reinsurance or as a derivative with standardised legal wording.
- If the swap is structured as reinsurance, then for both approaches, the contracts can be complicated;
- and so legal risk arises in relation to their interpretation. 

**Operational risk**
- There is operational risk for both approaches from the additional administrative tasks. 
- For example, the risk could arise from inadequate data or systems, e.g. errors in the transfer of data. 
- If the counterparty is a reinsurer, technical assistance may be provided;
- which may reduce the potential for operational risk.
- In both cases, the expenses incurred in setting up and managing the agreement may be higher than expected.
