<u><b>Q1(i): Describe how the assets would be valued under Solvency II.</u></b>
- Assets are taken at market value;
- based on readily available market prices;
- that are sourced independently.
- If prices are not available, mark-to-model techniques can be used.

<u><b>Q1(ii): Describe how the BEL would be calculated for annuities under Solvency II.</u></b>
- Need to project future cashflows;
- on a best estimate basis, with no prudential margins;
- allowing for all expected decrements and policyholder actions. 
- However, these will not include lapses;
- as policyholders are not expected to lapse from an annuity. 
- The cashflows will include all future expenses, including overheads; 
- and allowing for future expense inflation.
- The cashflows would then be discounted at the risk-free rate;
- allowing for the matching adjustment.
- Ideally the calculation would be performed on a policy-by-policy basis.
- The company should take account of all relevant available data, both internal and external, to best reflect the annuity portfolio.
- Contract boundaries are not relevant as there are no future premiums.
- A deterministic model is likely to be the most appropriate.

<u><b>Q1(iii): Identify the key risks related to the annuities for the company in the SCR.</u></b>
- There is longevity risk;
- where annuitants live longer than expected;
- and so annuities are paid for longer than expected.
- There is expense risk;
- where costs incurred are higher than expected.
- There is interest rate risk;
- e.g. where there is a mismatch between duration of assets and liabilities.
- There is risk of falling interest rates.
- It is likely that the duration of the annuity liabilities is longer than that of available assets and so an interest rate fall will increase liabilities by more than the increase in assets.
- There is a risk inflation is higher than expected.
- There is spread risk;
- e.g. from any corporate bonds used to match the liabilities.
- There is concentration risk;
- since the company is also exposed to the risk from concentrated exposure of any specific corporate bond used to back the annuities.
- There is operational risk;
- e.g. from errors in annuity payments.

<u><b>Q1(iv): Estimate the percentage change in BEL for the annuities under Country B relative to Country A.</u></b>
- Future cashflows are the same. 
- Best estimate assumptions used to derive the BEL are the same.
- The only differences are due to the yield curve, where it is on average 0.5% p.a. higher at all durations for Country B
- and the matching adjustment of 0.2% p.a. is not permitted in Country B.
- Therefore the discount rate will be only 0.5% - 0.2% = 0.3% p.a. higher within Country B. 
- With the average duration of 13 years, the liabilities will be approximately 13 * 0.3% = 3.9% lower.

<u><b>Q1(v): Contrast the SCR under Country B with the SCR under Country A for the annuities.</u></b>
- The longevity risk component of the SCR is lower. 
- The allowance for expense risk is lower as there is no inflation shock in Country B’s SCR.
- As the yield curve is higher under Country B, there is likely to be a difference in the interest rate risk.
- The higher yield will result in a lower discounted value of the liabilities
- resulting in a lower stress being applied.
- There is no matching adjustment so spread risk will be higher. 
- The allowance for operational risk is lower.
- The SCR will allow for diversification so there will be second order impacts.
- Also, as the tax regime is different, there may be an impact on the loss absorbing capacity of deferred taxes.
- Counterparty default risk will be higher;
- from the internal reinsurance arrangement.
- The overall impact on the SCR will depend on the combined effect of the above.

<u><b>Q1(vi): Justify whether the risk margin for the annuities under Country B will be higher or lower than County A.</u></b>
- Country B uses a higher risk discount rate;
- reducing the overall risk margin.
- The longevity risk component of the SCR is lower, implying a lower risk margin.
- The expense risk component of the SCR is lower, implying a lower risk margin.
- The operational risk component of the SCR is lower, implying a lower risk margin.
- The interest rate and spread risk will not have an impact;
- as the risk margin only considers the non-hedgeable risks.
- Overall the risk margin is expected to be lower.

<u><b>Q1(vii): Discuss other factors the company needs to consider before setting up the new subsidary.</u></b>
**Profitability** 
- The company will need to consider whether the venture meets its profit criteria. 
- It will need to consider the costs in setting up the subsidiary;
- as well as ongoing costs in managing the subsidiary overseas;
- e.g. hiring staff with specialist knowledge of Country B. 
- The profitability will be impacted by the potential tax benefits available in Country B.
- There may be restrictions on transferring profits between countries.

**Capital** 
- The company will need to consider the capital required at a group level. 
- There may be additional capital requirements in Country B;
- set by Country B’s regulator.
- Country B’s regulator may require the company to hold a parental guarantee over any subsidiary.
- Also, Country A may still require the company to hold extra capital at group level
- as there may be restrictions over capital fungibility between subsidiary and group.
- The profit should be compared with any impact on capital to determine whether the venture is worth undertaking.

**Risk** 
- It will be important to be confident with the regulation in Country B.
- This will also be important for any potential regulatory changes in Country A

**Practicalities and constraints**
- The company will need to consider the practicalities in setting up the subsidiary, e.g. the new systems.
- It will need to ensure it has the capabilities to transfer policy data securely.
- The company should consider whether the time and effort spent on setting up the subsidiary could have a negative impact on the company;
- for example whether it distracts senior management from its core business, e.g. ensuring compliance with the latest reserving requirements.
- Due to the different tax treatment, the company should consider whether it is able to gain the required specialist knowledge.
- The company should consider whether it will need to employ staff in Country B. 
- The company will have to follow another reporting regime.
- The company should consider whether there are any investment restrictions in Country B.

**Stakeholders** 
- The company will need to make sure it is continuing to treat its customers fairly. 
- It will want to consider the reaction of rating agencies.
- Country A’s regulator may not allow the transaction.
- Country B’s regulator may not allow the transaction
- e.g. it may not approve the new subsidiary.
- In order to assess whether the regulators from both countries will approve such a transaction, the company could look to see if there have been other companies carrying out a similar transaction.
- Consider whether there are any external reinsurance arrangements and, if so, whether the current reinsurer is happy with this proposed transaction.
