<u><b>Q2(i): Describe how the undiversified SCR is calculated for an individual risk.</b></u>
- Both assets and liabilities are stressed.
- The stress is calibrated to a 99.5% one-year Value-at-Risk.
- The SCR is the difference in net asset value between the unstressed scenario and the stressed scenario.
- The net asset value can be taken as assets less best estimate liabilities.

<u><b>Q2(ii): Discuss the risks that the company will hold capital for in the SCR.</b></u>
**Market risk**
- The stress will depend on the assets in which the company invests.
- There may be significant equity risks if the company invests in equities and depending on the size of the unit-linked business;
- as reduction in equity values would reduce the fund value and future fund-related charges.
- There may be significant interest rate risks as the company likely invests in bonds to back the pensions and annuity business.
- There may be significant credit spread risk but this depend on how the spread is allowed for via the matching adjustment.
- There may be some currency risk, but is unlikely to be significant.

**Insurance risk**
- There is unlikely to be significant mortality or morbidity risk, unless there are options or guarantees on the products that introduce this risk.
- There is likely significant longevity risk from the annuities, depending on the size of the annuity business.
- There is no lapse risk from the annuity business, but could be significant for the unit-linked business, as there is a risk to the profit stream from loss of future charges.
- There is likely significant expense risk:
    - for annuities, it depends on how expenses compare with the pricing assumptions;
    - for unit-linked business, it depends on how expenses compare with management charges.
- There will be counterparty default risk if there are reinsurance or outsourcing arrangements in place;
- which will depend on the level of exposure and the likelihood of default. 
- However, these arrangements may also reduce longevity risk and expense risk respectively.
- There will be operational risk (e.g. unit pricing errors, issues with outsourcing). 
- However, under standard formula, the capital requirement depends on the level of earned premiums and technical provisions.
- There is unlikely to be significant catastrophe risk.

<u><b>Q2(iii): Suggest possible reasons why the regulator might impose a capital add-on.</b></u>
- The regulator would have reviewed the SCR through Pillar 3 reports, such as RSR which includes ORSA.
- The regulator may conclude the risk profile is too different from that of a “standard” company for which the standard formula is designed for.
- For example:
	- the assets held may be atypical to a standard insurer;
	- the lives insured may be atypical to a standard insurer.
- Alternatively, the regulator may conclude that the risk assessment is inadequate.
- For example: 
	- there is concern on quality of data, e.g. poor quality hence requires additional prudence; 
	- there is concern on quality of ORSA;
	- there is concern on poor judgements in its risk assessment or capital calculation;
	- there is concern on the robustness of the overall risk management and governance framework.
- The regulator may impose a capital add-on to improve consistency with peer companies.
- The regulator may be planning to update the standard formula but has not yet done so (for example, updating catastrophe stresses following a recent pandemic).
- The regulator may find recent issues with the company that indicate or contribute to financial difficulties.

<u><b>Q2(iv): Suggest why the company may be required to change its internal model to incorporate this asset class.</b></u>
**Inclusion of new asset class**
- The new asset may be behaving significantly different to the rest of the assets to justify its own module. 
- The new asset may behave differently under stress.
- The new asset may a different payment structure. 
- The drivers for the change in asset value under stress may be different. 
- The variables that impact the asset value may not be stressed under the existing model.
- The correlation between risks may be different as a result of including the new asset class.
- If the asset reallocation is expected to be significant, the capital impact of an inappropriate stress would be material. 
- The regulator may have requested it.

<u><b>Q2(vi): Describe the requirements of APS X2 in relation to this new model development.</b></u>
**Review of actuarial work**
- APS X2 relates to review of actuarial work;
- which is work undertaken by someone with actuarial skills;
- for which the results are relied on. 
- The review should be done by someone with appropriate experience and expertise.
- The review includes an independent peer review. 
- The review should focus on where judgement has been made.
- The company likely has an internal process in place for independent review of methodologies.
- If an external company performs the model development, company may assume they carry out their own peer review.
- However, the company will still want to review so as to obtain comfort from any change.

<u><b>Q2(v): Outline the factors the company will need to consider when changing its internal model to incorporate the new asset class.</b></u>
**Data**
- How to access sufficient, high-quality data to set an appropriate stress for the new asset class.
- Whether relevant industry data is available.
- Input from investment managers, who are likely to hold valuable information on the new asset class.
- Whether sufficient historic data exists for the asset.
- All available sources of data should be considered, and the most appropriate and reliable sources selected.

**Assumptions and methodology**
- Which specific features or risk drivers of the new asset should be subject to stress testing.
- Where expert judgement will be required in setting assumptions.
- The new methodology must go through appropriate governance processes.
- The new methodology should be thoroughly tested to ensure it is fit for purpose.
- The company could validate the resulting capital requirement by comparing it against appropriate modules of the existing internal model or even the standard formula.
- Any changes to the model must be properly documented.

**Practicalities**
- Whether the company has sufficient internal expertise, or whether external support (e.g. from a consultancy) will be required.
- Whether the model can be simplified through the use of appropriate approximations.
- The time required for model development and its potential impact on reporting timelines.
- The complexity of the new model, as this could lead to long run times and put pressure on reporting deadlines.
- The overall cost of development and implementation, balanced against potential capital benefits or regulatory requirements.
- The company will need to obtain approval from the regulator for the model change.
