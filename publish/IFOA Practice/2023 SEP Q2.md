<u><b>Q3(i): Calculate the possible range of the MCR.</b></u>
- The Minimum Capital Requirement (MCR) has a floor of 25% of Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR)
- = 25% of €9m = €2.25m.
- The MCR has a cap of 45% of SCR
- = 45% of €9m = €4.05m.
- The MCR is subject to an overall minimum of €3.7m.
- so the possible range for the MCR is €3.7m to €4.05m.

<u><b>Q3(ii): Explain why the Board may have such concerns.</b></u>
- The first point of intervention is the failure to cover the SCR.
- In this case, the coverage of own funds over the SCR is 10/9 = 111%;
- which is not a strong position.
- There is requirement that only 15% of the SCR can be backed by Tier 3 capital;
- and at least 50% of the SCR must be backed by Tier 1 capital. 
- Tier 1 = €4.1m < 50% SCR = €4.5m;
- so the SCR is not covered by capital of sufficient quality;
- and there may already be some form of regulatory intervention;
- which may damage Company A’s reputation.
- There is a further requirement to cover the MCR. 
- Tier 3 capital cannot be used to cover the MCR
- but subordinated debt is Tier 3 capital.
- The only capital available to cover the MCR is the €4.1m of share capital.
- If the MCR is at the maximum level of €4.05m, then the coverage is only 101% 
- which is not a significant buffer;
- especially considering that failure to cover the MCR would result in loss of authorisation;
- which would have a severe impact on the company;
- and would possibly have consequences for Board members themselves.
- The regulator may have given the company an indication of acceptable limits (for the ratios of available capital to required capital).

- It may have made synergies from recent acquisitions

- and Co A may have been under consideration

<u><b>Q2(iv): Discuss the possible reasons why the SCR is not the sum of SCR of Company B prior to purchase and of Company A.</b></u>
- Unless Co B restated the SCR at year end, the SCR will be different as it is at a different point in time;

- market conditions will have changed;
- and the assets held may be different.

- or may have used its own actuarial models;
- both of which would change the stresses used to determine the SCR.
- Co A may have determined the SCR based on standard formula;
- and Co B may have determined the SCR based on internal model;
- and if Co B has not obtain approval for internal model, it may use a partial internal model.
- The correlation matrix for the combined company will differ to that of Co A giving a different SCR.
- The combined company may have greater diversification benefits;
- or better diversification across product ranges.
- Co B may be employing hedging of risks that were not available to Co A;
- or may be able to do this more efficiently / cheaply.

- Co B may take a different approach to the ability to reduce discretionary benefits of with-profits business.
- The loss absorbing capacity of deferred tax associated with the Co A business may differ for the combined company
- thus altering the loss absorbing capacity of technical provisions.
- Operational risk may have changed.
- There may have been errors in the calculation of one of the SCRs.

<u><b>Q2(v): Suggest possible reasons why Co B may maintain existing administration agreements of Co A or to migrate them.</b></u>
**Maintain**
- The administration systems inherited from Co A may be desirable.
- It may be that the administration systems of company A are efficient
- and may be one of the reasons that Co B purchased Co A.
- Co B may choose to migrate its with-profits endowments onto Co A’s in-house system.
- If the outsourcer company used by Co A is one that Co B already uses, then this may encourage Co B to leave the unit-linked business on that system.
- The cost of migration may be more than the benefits
- There may be specific features of Co A’s products which make future ongoing costs difficult to estimate
- or the migration may be difficult (and so not cost effective)
- or may want to defer migration until more stable and a view can be formed.
- For the unit-linked business, it should consider the remaining duration of the outsourcing agreement
- and depending on that term, Co B may choose to defer migrating the unit-linked business until the agreement has ended 
- or it may have to include the costs of breaking the agreement in the migration costs.

**Operational risk**
- Migration increases the risk that the system cannot administer policies in line with terms and conditions
- Transferring the data increases risk of data losses
- leading to rectification costs
- reputational damage 
- and possible regulatory sanction. 
- The results of the operational risk assessment may take priority over the financial considerations.

**Impact on customer service**
- Customer service may be damaged by migration
- e.g. management may be too busy with other business objectives
- so may not have sufficient resources for migration and maintain high quality customer service.

**Migrate**
- Co B may be able to simplify the process, based on past takeovers.
- The migration makes sense if the future savings in ongoing administration costs
- outweigh the costs of migration. 
- The cost-benefit analysis will rely on the assumptions made 
- on the future ongoing costs 
- and the project costs for the migration.
- Co B may believe that it can estimate these costs reasonably well.

**Impact on customer service**
- Co A may have lost key knowledgeable staff making it difficult to continue with its existing arrangements.
- Co B's admin systems may be able to provide customers with a better experience
- with more timely communications 
- and more insightful information
- enabling customers to make better decisions 
- or by providing customers with a greater choice of how to engage with the company.
