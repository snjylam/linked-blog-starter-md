<u><b>Q1(i): Discuss possible reasons why for the decline in the company's available regulatory capital.</u></b>
**Opening adjustments**
- The company may have changed its solvency valuation model.

**Return on opening surplus**
-  The market value of any assets backing the surplus assets may have decreased.
- The available capital could have fallen as a result of assets falling by more than technical provisions;
- and/or technical provisions increasing by more than assets.

**Experience variances**
- The decline in Available Regulatory Capital could be due to a fall in asset values, which hasn't been matched by a fall in the technical provisions;
- e.g. due to credit spreads widening;
- which has not been fully reflected in any matching or volatility adjustment.
- The assets and liabilities may not be well matched;
- e.g. due to not being able to find suitable bonds to match the annuities.
- Assets may have also fallen by more than the liabilities due to demographic experience being more adverse than expected;
- e.g. due to mortality experience being different to expected;
- e.g. higher for the term assurance products and lower for the annuity book.
- There is a natural mortality hedge between the annuity and term business.
- However, the latter will be sold to a different target market so the hedge is not perfect.
- The company has lots of experience at writing these products;
- so it is more likely the unexpected experience is due to an unexpected event;
- that doesn't impact both the term assurances and annuities equally;
- i.e. the negative impact on the term assurance is greater than the positive impact on the annuities.
- For example, it may be due to a pandemic.
- It could be due to the inexperience in writing the impaired life annuities;
- resulting in the company using an inappropriate mortality basis.
- Mortality experience variances will be minimised by any reinsurance the company has in place.
- Lapse experience on the term assurance policies may have been worse than expected;
- especially for early policy lapses
- Expense experience may be worse than expected;
- e.g. due to expense inflation being higher than expected;
- and/or any outsourcing or reinsurance in place increasing their fees.
- Investment return may have been lower than expected.
- There may have been more defaults than expected.

**Other variances**
- There may have been an unexpected increase in taxation over the period.
- There may have been an unexpected change in regulation over the period;
- e.g. a change to the cost of capital rate, increasing the technical provisions. 
- The company may have paid out more in dividends.
- The company may have incurred exceptional expenses over the three years;
- e.g. investing to improve efficiency in its systems;
- or to improve the control environment.

<u><b>Q1(iv): Explain why the company's rating agency capital may differ to the Available Regulatory Capital.</u></b>
- The definition of the value of assets and liabilities may differ between regulatory and rating agency assessments;
- so different methodology;
- and different assumptions.
- Rating agencies tend to use an economic capital approach, rather than regulatory;
- and may be more tailored to the individual company than the regulatory requirements.
- Rating agencies may use stochastic models.
- The value of assets may differ.
- For example, when valuing assets not traded on a regulated market.
- It aims to be a realistic capital requirement;
- rather than a requirement to protect solvency.
- For term and annuity business the technical provisions are realistic;
- and deterministic;
- discounted at risk free;
- with some allowance for liquidity;
- plus, the cost of holding the SCR/risk margin.
- Also, actions taken to reduce the SCR may mean a loss of economic value.
- For example, if this company uses reinsurance;
- which may not have the same impact on regulatory liabilities.
- The company's risk appetite framework will play a part in the difference between the two.
