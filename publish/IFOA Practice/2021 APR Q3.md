<u><b>Q3(ii): Describe briefly the standard formula approach under Solvency II.</b></u>
**Standard formula appraoch**
- The standard formula is based on a one-year Value-at-Risk measure;
- at the 99.5% confidence level.
- There is a defined set of risks for which capital must be held.
- For each of these risks; the standard formula prescribes a specific stress test; 
- with detailed rules relating to the calibration and application of each stress test.
- The capital required for each individual risk is the difference between the net asset value in the unstressed balance sheet and the net asset value in the stressed balance sheet.
- These individual capital amounts are combined across the risks within a module, using a specified correlation matrix.
- A further specified correlation matrix is used to combine capital across modules.
- Capital is added for operational risk;
- which is calculated based on percentages of earned premiums and technical provisions.
- An adjustment may be made for the loss absorbing capacity of the technical provisions;
- which could include the ability to reduce discretionary benefits under stressed conditions.

<u><b>Q3(iii): Describe briefly the internal model approach under Solvency II.</b></u>
**Internal model approach**
- An internal model allows the company to tailor the solvency capital assessment to its own risk profile.
- It must cover (at least) the risk types that are defined for the standard formula.
- The use of an internal model must be approved by the regulator.
- As part of the approval process insurers must demonstrate that they meet certain tests and standards
- These include the following:
	- use test to show that the model is widely used within the company for decision-making and other processes;
	- statistical quality standards to show that minimum standards are met for assumption setting and data;
	- calibration standards to show that the assessment is equivalent to a 99.5% confidence one-year Value-at-Risk;
	- profit and loss attribution to show how the categorisation of risk chosen is used to explain the sources of profit and loss;
	- validation standards to show that the model has been fully validated and regularly reviewed;
	- documentation standards.
- Allowance should be made for non-linearity and non-separability of risks.
- Rather than using stress tests combined with correlation matrices, stochastic simulations could be used – perhaps using copulas to model dependencies between risks.
- Calibration of the model must be done with care, particularly when setting probability distributions and dependencies that apply under extreme conditions.
- An internal model can allow for the impact of dynamic hedging.
- A partial internal model may be used, with the standard formula being used for some risks (e.g. operational risk).

<u><b>Q3(iv): Suggest possible reasons why it is reasonable for there to be two approaches to determine capital requirements.</b></u>
**Reasons for offering the standard formula approach**
- The regulator is likely to offer this approach for practical reasons.
- Smaller companies might not have the expertise;
- or available resources to implement an internal model.
- The cost of developing an internal model might be prohibitive for these smaller companies.
- There is also less work involved for the regulator under this approach.
- There is also less cost for the regulator.
- It may not have the resources available to approve an internal model for every insurance company. 

**Reasons for offering the internal model approach**
- The standard formula is only appropriate for companies with an 'average' risk profile. 
- The standard formula might not fit with the risk profile of the company;
- e.g. it is a non-typical company.
- For some companies, the standard formula would result in a disproportionately high amount of required capital.
- e.g. for larger companies, the extra implementation cost of an internal model may be justified by lower capital requirements.
- or a well-managed company with effective internal risk management processes in place;
- or for a very simple company.
- However, the standard formula might also result in some companies being under-capitalised. 
- The regulator would not want the industry to be unnecessarily over-capitalised through using a broadbrush approach.

**Overall** 
- The use of two approaches balances effective prudential capital requirements;
- with an appropriate regulatory cost for the industry. 

<u><b>Q3(v): Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of each option from the viewpoint of the regulator and insurer.</b></u>
**Transition in for all business**
**Advantages**
- This may be better for the regulator as all business is assessed under the improved regime, giving them more meaningful information.
- There is a more predictable transition to the new regime than under the second approach;
- since the run-off of the transitional adjustments is likely to be over a specified term.
- Allowing for a transitional adjustment, rather than bringing the full change in immediately, is beneficial for insurance companies;
- and for the regulator, as it preserves market stability;
- and is more likely to improve confidence in the industry.
- This approach is more likely to gain granting of equivalence with Solvency II, which may be a regulatory objective.
- All additional reporting using the new regime (e.g. any ORSA or equivalent) will be consistent across the whole business.
- The risks inherent in more complex existing contracts will be better reflected in capital requirements under this approach.

**Disadvantages**
- Some insurance companies may be insolvent under this approach;
- even with the transitional adjustments in place.
- Insolvencies would disrupt the market.
- The transitional adjustments require additional model development work.
- It may be difficult to interpret the new rules for some legacy business.
- The data may not exist for valuing some legacy business under the new rules.

**Introduce for new business only**
**Advantages**
- Introducing the proposed requirements for new business only is likely to be easier for the insurance companies;
- and so may be cheaper for them to implement.
- It is also likely to be easier for the regulator.
- This approach may mean less strain (at least initially) on resources.
- This would be important if the insurance company has other resource pressures at the same time, e.g. due to the implementation/embedding of new accounting requirements such as IFRS.
- The run-off of the previous regime is over the life of the existing business;
- which companies might find to be more appropriate.
- There is more time for companies to refine their models;
- and for the regulator to resolve any issues in the regulations.
- This approach might help the regulator to focus on potential issues with the regime for new product designs.

**Disadvantages**
- This approach requires multiple valuation processes to be in place.
- There will be additional complexity if statutory disclosures differ between the two blocks of business.
- Having two valuation processes running in parallel increases the ongoing complexity for companies;
- and for the regulator.

<u><b>Q3(viii): Outline the risks in respect of the unit-linked endownment described above that would contribute to Company A's SCR.</b></u>
**Market risk**
- The company is exposed to market risk; which is the risk that as a result of market movements, there is fluctuations in value of assets and liabilities;
- specifically it is the risk of low investment returns;
- reducing the value of the unit fund and charges received. 
- There may also be the following risks, subject to the assets held in the unit fund:
	- equity risk;
	- property risk;
	- interest rate risk;
	- spread risk;
	- currency risk;
	- concentration risk.
- These are likely to contribute significantly to the SCR.

**Insurance risk**
- There is mortality risk due to the 1% additional death benefit;
- and in early durations, early death may lead to non-recovery of initial expenses;
- and there being no penalty imposed on the death benefit. 
- The mortality risk is not likely to contribute significantly to the SCR.
- There is persistency risk which is risk of increased withdrawals;
- leading to loss of future charges;
- The persistency risk is likely to contribute significantly to the SCR.
- There is expense risk which is the risk of higher than expected expenses;
- and the risk of higher than expected expense inflation.
- The persistency risk is likely to contribute moderately to the SCR;
- although the extent of expense risk depends on any outsourcing arrangements in place.
- There is a risk of lower new business volumes than expected; 
- which increases per policy expenses and inability to meet overheads.

**Other risks**
- There is counterparty default risk, in relation to outsourcing arrangements. 
- There is liquidity risk, e.g. in relation to any direct property held in the unit fund.
- There is operational risk, e.g. in relation to unit pricing.
