<u><b>Q1(i): Discuss the practical challenges and potential solutions for the opening balance sheet.</u></b>
**Roll forward from normal valuation date** 
- Producing reserves at a non-standard valuation date may create practical and operational challenges.
- The insurer will need to consider how to extract policyholder valuation data;
- and asset data.
- The systems may not be able to extract data at the required date, so a practical approach may be needed.
- For example, data could be extracted at a standard valuation date and then rolled forward, adjusting for movements after the extraction date, such as new policies written, benefits paid, premiums received.
- Alternatively, if the required balance sheet date is close to a standard valuation date:
- the insurer could use data from the nearest available valuation date;
- but must check for any significant market movements between the two dates.

**Assumptions and data considerations**
- The insurer will need to consider how to set assumptions at a different valuation date.
- It should assess whether relevant data (e.g. market data) is available at that date.
- If not, it must determine whether assumptions from the previous valuation date remain appropriate.

**Model and operational limitations**
- There is a risk that actuarial models cannot handle non-standard valuation dates.
- In such cases, reserves may need to be:
    - calculated at a standard valuation date; and
    - rolled forward or backward, 
    - allowing for policyholder movements and market changes.
- Company A could use management information (MI) to adjust for actual experience, such as claims.
- A balance must be struck between:
    - accuracy; and
    - cost and practicality.
- For example, the insurer should consider whether a simplified roll-forward approach is sufficiently robust.

**Implementation and governance considerations**
- Company A will need to assess how its current valuation approach compares to the Group’s methodology.
- It may need to update actuarial models to align with Group reporting requirements.
- This could involve calculating new types of reserves;
- or adopting different assumption-setting rules.
- The Board of Company A may need training on the new reporting basis so that they understand the results. 
- Company A may lack internal expertise;
- however, Group-level resources should be available to support implementation.

<u><b>Q1(IiI): Discuss the potential benefits of the transfer for Company B.</u></b>
**Practical considerations**
- It will simplify the reporting process;
- as Company B does not need to produce separate reports for each fund.
- It may result in cost savings;
- and improved profitability;
- especially if the processes can be simplified / aligned.
- It may result in lower probability of errors in the process.
- It will reduce the number of funds (from five to two);
- which may reduce the required manpower on the reserving calculations;
- leading to headcount savings.
- It may reduce the total number of committees, which will also reduce costs.
- Data will not be needed to be split with as much granularity (i.e. the data only needs to be split into two funds instead of five).
- Overhead costs will be spread across more policies leading to economies of scale.
- Assumptions could be aligned which may simplify the process.
- For future methodology changes the company only needs to change one with-profits (without-profits) model instead of two (three).

**Technical considerations**
- There may be tax benefits for the Company B.
- There may be increased risk diversification;
- which may reduce the overall capital requirements.
- There may be investment management benefits from pooling funds (e.g. enabling direct investment into equity and property instead of collective investment schemes);
- and improved liquidity.

<u><b>Q1(Iv): Discuss the areas covered in the independent expert's report and implications for the policyholder.</u></b>
**Policyholder benefits and expectations**
- Without profits policyholders of Company A and B expect no changes to their benefits.
- Transferring with profits policyholders of Company A expect future bonus to be in line with past bonuses.
- Existing with profits policyholders of Company B also expect future bonus to be in line with past bonuses.
- The IE should consider the impact of the transfer on bonus strategy (split between regular and terminal);
- the smoothing policy;
- the surrender value;
- and ensure that policyholders are not worse off by the transfer.
- It may be necessary to provide policyholders some gains to ensure no one is worse off.
- If there is an inherited estate in either funds, policyholder's expectations on these should be considered;
- which may require a distribution of the estate prior to the transfer of liabilities.
- The IE should consider the PPFM of Company A.
- The IE should consider the impact to investment strategy.
- The IE should consider the impact to customer service.

**Security of policyholder benefits**
- The IE should ensure the capital projection is not worse after the transfer than pre transfer.
- The IE will need to see the capital projections;
- on a range of scenarios;
- covering different economic assumptions;
- and different demographic assumptions.
- The IE should ensure the company solvency is not at risk after the transfer;
- to ensure future policyholder benefits can be met;
- for policyholders in both companies.
- The IE may consider whether the Group parent company provides capital support.
- The IE may consider the impact of the transfer on persistency rates;
- and when persistency would be expected to return to normal levels. 

**TCF**
- The IE will need to consider TCF impacts for each groups of policyholders separately;
- to ensure each group of policyholders is treated fairly post transfer.
- The IE will need to consider fair treatment between policyholders and shareholders, e.g.  there may be a difference in the way that the surplus is currently split in the two funds.
- Without profits charges should be in line with TCF.
- The IE will need to consider how to communicate the potential impacts (expectations, security and TCF) to policyholders.

<u><b>Q1(v): Discuss the implications of  offering tax incentives in line with the tax relief on premiums paid into the UK pension products.</u></b>
- The government may provide tax relief on premiums, it would incentivise people to pay into their pensions;
- rather than other investments;
- as they invest the gross amount into the pension but only the net amount in other types of savings;
- particularly for higher tax rate earners.
- It may lead to increased new business;
- and may increase profits;
- as more premiums are received;
- and improve solvency coverage ratio;
- but may lead to risk of increased new business strain.
- The company may want to reprice to reflect the consequences of higher levels of new business. 
- The government may place restrictions on surrendering, so surrenders may reduce.
- The government may limit the maximum amount that policyholders can invest under the new incentive;
- and the company may need to reclaim tax from the government;
- which may both result in operational issues for the company. 
- There may be implications for administration systems;
- and other systems and actuarial models;
- and actuarial data (e.g. the target market of the product might change);
- and need to train staff on the tax changes.
- The changes may impact policyholder illustrations;
- especially if the change only impacts future premiums;
- which may impact lapse and re-entry risk;
- which will mean more complications for systems;
- as will need to separate premiums and investments pre and post the change.
- There may be operational risk for the company, for example reclaiming tax from the Government.
