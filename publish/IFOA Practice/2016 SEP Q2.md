<u><b>Q2(i): State the key objectives of Solvency II.</b></u>
- To increase the level of harmonisation of solvency regulation for insurers across EU in terms of:
	- capital adequacy assessment;
	- risk management;
	- reporting.
- To protect policyholders.
- To introduce a more risk-based capital adequacy assessment approach.

<u><b>Q2(ii): Describe the Solvency II Pillar 3 reporting requirements.</b></u>
**Private supervisory reporting** 
- The Regulatory Supervisory Report (RSR) needs to be disclosed privately to the supervisor, which includes:
	- Solvency I Pillar 1 solvency calculations;
	- Solvency II Pillar 2 ORSA;
	- other qualitative information, e.g. details on company's risk management processes. 
- The Quantitative Reporting Templates (QRT) are used to disclose quantitative information in a prescribed format.
- The RSR and QRT needs to be submitted annually; 
- although under certain conditions a summary (material changes) RSR is acceptable.
- A subset of QRT needs to be submitted quarterly to support the MCR calculation.

**Public supervisory reporting** 
- Except for certain items that are confidential in nature;
- extracts from QRT and RSR are disclosed public Solvency and Financial Condition Report (SFCR);
- which is produced annually.
- The local regulators  are permitted to impose additional reporting requirements in the form of ‘national specific templates’;
- which address specific requirements of the local country.

<u><b>Q2(iii): Describe how the company would determine the Solvency II technical provisions for the unit-linked product.</b></u>
**Method**
- The technical provisions consist of a best estimate liability (BEL) and a risk margin (RM). 
- As the product is unit-linked, the unit and non-unit components may be unbundled for the purposes of determining the technical provisions. 

**BEL – Unit reserve calculation**
- The BEL for the unit reserves should be based on the unit price and the number of units the company needs to hold.
- This should be derived from the unit prices on the valuation date.

**BEL – Non-unit reserve calculation** 
**Technical Provisions**
- The non-unit liabilities are the present value of expected future cashflows. 
- The cashflows are discounted using Solvency II risk-free yield curve issued by EIOPA for the relevant currency. 
- The cashflows will include:
	- expenses;
	- less premiums not allocated to units;
	- less the fixed charges (i.e. of x% p.a. varying by fund).
- The future expenses needs to consider both overheads and directly attributable expenses;
- no reserve is required for the expenses of closure.
- The future premiums are taken up to the ‘contract boundary’;
- which is the point at which a company can unilaterally terminate the contract;
- or refuse to accept a premium;
- or reprice or alter benefits so they fully reflect the risks.
- For this contract, the contract boundary is likely to be the selected retirement date of the policyholder. 
- The future charges can be determined by projecting the unit fund.
- The cashflow projections should ideally be performed on a policy-by-policy basis;
- but model points can be used provided certain conditions are met;
- including validation of accuracy.
- The non-unit reserve can be negative. 

**Assumptions**
- The investment return assumptions should be risk-free.
- The non-investment return assumptions should be best estimate with no prudential margins.
- The non-unit reserve projections should allow for all expected decrements and policyholder actions, i.e. they should allow for full surrenders, partial withdrawals and death. 

**BEL – Guaranteed annuity option** 
**Technical provisions**
- The GAO may be calculated separately. 
- The company may use a stochastic approach to calculate the part of BEL required to cover the expected cost of GAO;
- although a deterministic ‘closed form’ solution could be acceptable depending on the risks.
- The stochastic simulation would require a large number of projections to be run.
- The cost of guarantee is determined for each simulation as projected unit fund at retirement multiplied by the higher of zero and the present value of an annuity;
- where the annuity rate is based on the difference between the guaranteed rate and the simulated market annuity rate.
- The BEL would be the average value over all of the simulated liabilities;
- discounted at the Solvency II risk-free yield curve.
- The cost of the guarantee will likely be carried out on a policy-by-policy basis.
- This reduces the cross subsidies between policies that are ‘in the money’ and ‘out of the money’ at the option date.

**Assumptions**
- The unit fund would be projected using best estimate assumptions for possible decrements.
- The model should allow for: 
	- appropriate probability distributions;
	- allowing for different volatilities;
	- and correlations.
- The model should be market-consistent, so parameters should be calibrated to observed market values;
- e.g. the market annuity rate should from an observed open market annuity simulated using risk-free interest rates. 
- The interest rates / investment return rates should be modelled stochastically;
- and consistent with the EIOPA Solvency II risk-free yield curve.
- The expense inflation should be modelled stochastically; 
- and correlated to the interest rate / investment return.
- For both the non-unit reserve model and the COG model, allowance is needed for projection of best estimate longevity;
- allowing for future mortality improvements. 
- The option take-up rate assumption should be best estimate;
- and will only be required for those polices with the option.
- It is unlikely that all policyholders will take the option even if ‘in the money’, so should be set at less than 100%.

**Risk margin** 
- The risk margin is determined using a cost of capital approach;
- i.e. based on the cost of holding capital to support those risks that cannot be hedged.
- These include all insurance risk, reinsurance credit risk, operational risk and ‘residual market risk’.
- For this contract the non-hedgeable risks are likely to be:
	- persistency;
	- longevity risk in relation to the guaranteed annuity rate;
	- operational risk.
- The risk margin calculation requires a projection of the future capital that the company is required to hold at the end of each projection period (e.g. year) during the run-off of the existing business.
- The projected capital requirement is a subset of the SCR, consisting of those risks that cannot be hedged in financial markets.
- These projected capital amounts are then multiplied by a cost of capital rate;
- which is 6% p.a. for Solvency II.
- The product of the cost of capital rate and the capital requirement at each future projection point is then discounted, using risk-free discount rates, to give the overall risk margin. 
- As projection of the SCR is complex, simplified approaches can be used;
- for example, selecting a driver which has an approximately linear relationship with the required capital or its components;
- e.g., policy count for expense risk.
- The initial capital requirement can be expressed as a percentage of that driver and the projected capital is then approximated as the same percentage of the projected values of the driver.
- The risk margin can be reduced to take into account diversification with different lines of business;
- e.g. with the immediate annuity product. 

**Discount rate**
- As the liabilities within the unit-linked savings product are not predictable due to customers able to surrender at any time, the company will not be able to apply a matching adjustment; 
- but it may be possible to apply a volatility adjustment to the risk-free yield curve;
- which requires approval from the regulator.
- The volatility adjustment is based on the spreads on a representative portfolio of assets for each relevant currency.

<u><b>Q2(iv): Assess the impacts of legislation removing mandate to purchase immediate annuity for policies without GAO.</b></u>
**Market conditions**
- The legislation allows more options for policyholders at retirement.
- As a result, the annuity take-up rate is expected to fall. 
- In the short term, the annuity take-up rate may be volatile and it may take some time for the long-term impact to be clear. 
- If market annuity rates are poor value for money; this would result in fewer policyholders taking an annuity. 
- The annuity take-up rate also depends on the availability of alternatives to annuity products, e.g. income drawdown.
- The greater the availability of alternatives, the less likely the policyholder will take the annuity.
- Any negative reputation of insurance companies will increase the likelihood that the policyholder will take the cash and invest elsewhere.

**Target market**
- Some policyholder may prefer the lump sum cash;
- for example to go on an expensive holiday or repay an outstanding loan. 
- The lower the level of the fund, the more likely the remaining customers take the remaining amount as cash.
- Some policyholders may not be financially sophisticated and may lack financial advice;
- and as such may not have fully understood the options available;
- and have failed to understand the more cash taken now, the higher the risk of not being able to fund themselves in later life.
- Between standard and impaired lives, impaired lives are more likely to take the cash option.

**Tax / Regulation**
- The annuity take-up rate depends on tax levels and any tax allowances. 
- Policyholders may not appreciate that by taking the fund value as cash, they incur more tax than if they took the annuity;
- i.e. there may be a large tax bill for the policyholder in the year they retire as opposed to being taxed by a lower rate of income tax from the future annuity payments. 
- The annuity take-up rate depends on availability of State provision in retirement. 
- If this is high, it is more likely that policyholders will take the cash;
- as they may feel the risk of living longer than their savings can provide for has already been met by State provisions.

<u><b>Q2(v): Assess how the potential impact would be different for policies that have the GAO?</b></u>
**Onerousness of the guarantee**
- The take-up rates will mainly depend on whether the guarantee is in the money and if so to what extent.
- If the GAO is very heavily in the money, the take-up rate should be similar to that during the previous legislation;
- i.e. it is very likely policyholders would exercise the option. 
- If the GAO is less heavily in the money, the take-up rate depends on the policyholders’ awareness of the guarantee;
- and understanding of its higher value. 
- However, even if policyholder understands the value, some policyholders may prefer cash even if it is the less valuable option.
- For example, if driven by policyholder's personal circumstances.
- If the GAO is not in the money, the take-up rate would be similar to those policies without the option.
- The rate at which policyholders take the tax-free lump sum should remain unaffected by the legislation change.
- It may take some time for the annuity take-up experience to stabilise and for the long-term impact to be determined.
