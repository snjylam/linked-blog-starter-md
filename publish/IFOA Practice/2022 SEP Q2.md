<u><b>Q2(i): Describe the possible impact that the fall in new with-profits business may have for both policyholders and shareholders.</b></u>
**General** 
- The impact will depend on whether the fall in new business continues.
- However, given it has been steadily declining, it is likely the trend will continue.
- The company may have expected this trend 
- and so may have in place procedures it will want to follow.
- Before the company changes anything, it will want to compare actual with expected experience.
- If the experience is not expected to continue, the impact will likely be minimal. 
- The company may change its focus to the without-profits business
- possibly leading to higher operational risk exposure on its with-profits business. 
- Or it may decide to sell the with-profits business to a third party.
- Alternatively the company may be distracted by what is going on with its with-profits business
- which may adversely impact the without-profits business. 
- The company may decide to outsource the running of the with-profits fund.

**Policyholders** 
**Asset share**
- Per policy asset shares may fall in value
- as per policy expenses may have increased
- due to fewer policies being left to share the fixed expenses.
- The final maturity payout may therefore no longer meet its intended purpose.
- The impact on policyholders of increasing per policy expenses will depend on whether fixed charges or actual expenses are deducted from the asset share.
- If the former, policyholders shouldn’t be impacted by the increased per policy expenses
- unless the company decides to increase charges to asset shares 
- for example, if the fixed charges were for a fixed duration.
- Higher lapses may result from any erosion in policy value.

**Investment strategy**
- The company may decide to move to safer investments if it feels that the free assets of the with-profits fund will deplete and not be sufficient to back risky investments.
- As claims outweigh new business premiums, the company may face liquidity issues
- and as a result, it may decide to reduce direct property investment.
- The company may have to sell property at poor prices due to the contraction of the fund.

**Bonuses** 
- If the trend continues
- the company may decide to change its bonus policy
- e.g. move to lower terminal bonuses so as to match the change in investment strategy.

**Smoothing**
- As the fund depletes, the company may reduce the extent of smoothing.

**The estate**
- The company may decide to start to distribute the estate
- so as not to risk a tontine effect
- Policyholders may benefit from this distribution via an increase in bonuses. 
- Policyholders may see an increase in any estate distribution due to fewer policyholders to share the estate amongst.
- There is likely to be a positive impact on policyholders from the estate distribution if the company makes distributions in a timely manner.
- If the asset share doesn’t normally include estate additions, then policyholders may appear better off.
- If it delays, this will likely lead to inequality and the unfair treatment of policyholders. 
- If it delays, this will be unfair between policyholders leaving the fund in the near future 
- compared to those who remain in-force for a long duration.

**Shareholders**
- Lower new business will likely reduce shareholder transfers in future due to lower future bonuses.
- The extent will depend on the split of profits between policyholders and shareholders
- e.g. if it is a 90:10 with-profits fund, shareholders will receive at least 10% of any distribution. 
- The fall in new business is likely to adversely impact the company’s reputation amongst shareholders
- Lower bonuses will reduce the value of the with-profits business to shareholders. 
- Shareholders may have to support the with-profits fund
- e.g. by capital injections
- if there is a risk of insolvency, etc. 
- Shareholder transfers may therefore be reduced. 
- Any negative market reception will likely reduce the marketability of the company’s shares. 
- Rating agencies may downgrade ratings
- adversely impacting financing.

<u><b>Q2(ii): Explain why set BEL to be asset share plus COG is reasonable for the BEL.</b></u>
**Why asset share?** 
- The asset share will already be calculated.
- Policyholders will expect their claim payment to equal asset share;
- as communicated in policyholder literature.
- With-profits claim payments will consist of guaranteed payments, i.e. sum assured plus any attaching bonuses;
- plus discretionary future bonuses.
- The BEL is the expected cashflows on the policy discounted at the risk free rate.
- The main cashflow projected in the BEL is the claim payout.
- Therefore the BEL should resemble the asset share, where asset share is above the guarantees. 
- The surrender value at the valuation date will be based on the asset share.

**Why cost of guarantees?**
- However, the guarantees could be greater than the asset share at the point of claim;
- and the BEL therefore needs to take these unknown future amounts into account.
- Some with-profits policies may have additional guarantees;
- e.g. any guaranteed annuity options;
- or additional mortality guarantees.
- The cost of these guarantees and options are likely to be modelled stochastically.
- The calculation of the cost of guarantees should allow for the future premiums payable (as these offset some of the cost).
- The asset share alone will not be enough to cover any planned estate distribution. 
- The suggested approach doesn’t allow for the cost of smoothing.
- However, if the company recycles these within the fund, the smoothing cost may be minimal. 

<u><b>Q2(iii): Discuss the impact of experience on the estate.</b></u>
**Higher mortality** 
- Claims on death should equal asset share
- so higher death claims will have little impact on the estate.
- However, this will depend on the duration of the policy at the date of death
- e.g. it is likely that early deaths will pay out an amount greater than asset share.
- This cost could be charged to the estate.
- Or it could be charged to the remaining policies in-force
- causing the total asset share of in-force to fall
- resulting in an increase in the overall cost of guarantees.
- If higher mortality rates are expected to continue, the company may update mortality assumptions
- increasing the future cost of mortality guarantees.
- However, there are now fewer policies expected to mature
- reducing the future cost of maturity guarantees
- The BEL will be released on death, so the closer the BEL is to asset share, the smaller the impact on the estate.
- Any cost of guarantee component of the BEL will be released
- increasing the estate.
- The overall impact on the estate will depend on how the release compares with the increase in the cost of guarantees (from lower assets shares).

**Equity falls**
- Asset share will fall to the extent that equities are backing the asset share. 
- This will increase the cost of guarantees.
- Any increase in the cost of guarantees will result in a fall in the estate
- unless the estate is hedged by derivatives.
- The estate will also see a fall in value
- to the extent that it is invested in equities.
- As liabilities (asset share) will fall along with the assets, the change in asset share should result in a neutral impact on the estate.
- However, liabilities also include the cost of guarantees component.
- If the estate is not backed by equity then the only impact will be from the higher cost of guarantees
- and if the estate is hedged with respect to equity falls, then there could be very little impact on the estate.
- If this fall is likely to continue, the company may decide to update the assumptions
- e.g. the equity volatility assumption
- used to value the cost of guarantees.

<u><b>Q2(iv): Suggest actiions the company could take to maintain the fund while remaining open to new business.</b></u>
**Investigations** 
- The company should perform an investigation to identify what is causing the poor experience;
- and then to consider ways to manage the poor experience;
- including promoting new business.
- However, if new business levels are falling across the with-profits market then this may be challenging.
- To reduce costs, it will want to manage funds more efficiently;
- e.g. reduce expenses.
- The actions taken will depend on whether the company thinks the poor experience is likely to persist.
- If experience is expected to persist, the company may need to update assumptions in its models .
- e.g. to show how the recent poor experience will impact solvency in its valuation models. 
- New business could be promoted by spending more on advertising;
- or building better relationships with intermediaries;
- possibly offering more competitive premiums.
- However if with-profits is now considered ‘old-fashioned’, this may reflect poorly on the company’s reputation.
