<u><b>Q3(i): Describe three ways a life insurance company might be taxed on its business.</b></u>
- Taxation on profits (the excess of the change in value of assets less the change in value of liabilities).
- Taxation on investment income less expenses.
- Taxation on premium income.

<u><b>Q3(ii): Discuss the suitability of each of these methods of taxation for a term assurance product.</b></u>
**Taxation on profits**
- This is likely suitable.
- Although term assurance is not likely to generate profits in the early years;
- so there is a deferral in tax relief;
- to even out taxable profits.
- This may be complex for the insurer and tax authority.

**Taxation on investment income less expenses**
- This is likely not suitable.
- The reserves for term assurance are low;
- so investment income is low;
- relative to expenses;
- so tax may be low;
- or negative;
- which would not be desirable for the tax authority.
- It will be difficult for companies who only write term assurance to compete;
- with companies who write XSI products;
- as the latter is able to allow for tax relief on their term assurance expenses in pricing.
- This may be complex for the insurance company. 

**Taxation on premium income**
- This can be argued as suitable or not suitable.
- This is simple;
- for both insurer and tax authority.
- This approach could be argued as fair as all companies are treated the same way.
- This taxable amount is predictable;
- by the insurer and the tax authority.
- However, companies may pass the taxation to policyholders in the form of higher premiums;
- leading to lower volumes sold;
- and lower profit for the company. 
- In all three cases, the impact to policyholders will also depend on how their benefits are taxed.

<u><b>Q3(iii): Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of launching individual term assurance as first product offering.</b></u>
**Advantages - Profit / Risk / Capital**
**Practicalities**
- The product is simple so development costs should be low. 
- The product has clearly meets customer needs.
- Term assurance is simple to sell and explain, and cheap to purchase. 
- The product can be sold with many distribution methods available depending on the target market.
- High volumes can potentially be sold via direct marketing (via the internet) to the mass market.
- Administration could be outsourced at competitive prices as it is a simple product. 
- There may be possibilities for cross-selling with niche rider benefits.
- There are potential tax advantages, i.e. if expenses can be used to gain tax relief and so can be netted down in the premium calculations.
- Reinsurers should be able to provide expertise on pricing/underwriting.
- Reinsurance should be easy to obtain to manage mortality risk.
- Capital requirements for term assurance may be lower than for other products. 
- Under a best estimate basis, reserves are likely to be negative. 
- The product is simple to launch and easy to administer as a first offering. 
- There may be a high supply of technical skills, experts/consultants in relation to underwriting. 

**Disadvantages - Profit / Risk / Capital / Practicalities**
- The product is price sensitive and usually has a competitive market; 
- so it may be difficult to achieve the required profit margin;
- and price competitively against large insurers. 
- The company may not have any existing mortality data.
- The company may not have expertise in understanding and managing mortality risk.
- This may lead to inappropriate pricing or underwriting;
- leading to lower profits than expected. 
- If business volumes are too low, the company may not cover its fixed expenses, e.g. set up underwriting systems;
- and will have volatile claims experience and uncertain profits.
- If business volumes are too high, there may be stress on administration; leading to negative impact on reputation. 
- If business volumes are too high, there may be high capital strain; e.g. due to commission and underwriting costs. 
- There is significant expense risk as  a relatively large proportion of premium covers expenses;
- especially in early durations as initial expenses are high and there is a risk of early lapses.
- There is significant anti-selection risk.
- There is significant mortality risk. 
- External mortality data may not be relevant to the intended target market. 
- There may be complex systems development requirements, e.g. underwriting or taxation of this product.

<u><b>Q3(v): Determine the company's total tax charge for the year, covering both the policyholder and shareholder share of profits.</b></u>
- I = 50 + 30 + 70 = 150
- I - E = 150 - 60 = 90
- Shareholder's share = 42
- Policyholder's share = 90 - 42 = 48
- Tax on shareholder's share = 42 * 19% = 7.98
- Tax on policyholder's share = 48 * 20% = 9.60
- Total tax = 7.98 + 9.60 = 17.58

<u><b>Q3(vii): Discuss why Company A would sell the block of investment business.</b></u>
**Profit**
- The investment product may not meet the shareholders’ required return 
- and so is not turning out to be profitable.
- The investment product is not achieving the desired economies of scale. 
- The company may not compete in the investment product market and so unable to sell sufficient volumes. 
- The company may not have sold a sufficient volumes to recoup the high launch and other fixed costs and wishes to exit before this situation worsens. 
- Company A may not have built sufficient internal expertise in this type of business. 
- Company B may have offered a good price. 
- Company B may be willing to pay extra for the brand.
- Company B may have some regulatory or tax arbitrage benefit over Company A
- If volumes were lower than expected, the tax basis may have differed from what was allowed for in the pricing, e.g. if the company remains in an XSE position.

**Risk**
- The company may not have been able to meet the customers’ needs
- leading to customer complaints. 
- It may therefore have decided to sell the business to avoid further reputational damage 
- and so protect sales of its other products
- The regulator may have enforced the sale. 
- The company may be anticipating an adverse regulatory / tax change. 

**Capital**
- The company may need more capital 
- to be able to develop the product.
- The company may not be prepared to hold as much locked-in capital as is needed for this business.
- Any guarantees attached to the product may have proved to be more onerous than expected. 
- Any complaints may have led to fines. 
- The regulator may have increased the capital required to be held against the product. 

**Practicalities / operations** 
- The product may no longer fit in with the company’s business strategy. 
- The target market may be completely different to that of the existing term assurance product, 
- and so the company may have needed to set up a different distribution channel. 
- There may have then been a relationship breakdown with this new channel, 
- or any outsourcing arrangement, 
- such as investment management, admin, etc. 
- Or there may have been a breakdown within the company’s own internal processes.
