<u><b>Q2(i): Describe the standard components in the analysis of change that would relate to investment return.</b></u>
**Return on net assets**
- This reflects the actual investment return earned on the starting net assets. 
- The return on net assets may be split between the expected investment return (risk-free) and the excess of expected investment return over actual.

**Expected return on in-force business**
- This is the expected change in the value of the in-force business over the year
- calculated on the economic assumptions at the beginning of the year. 
- The expected return on in-force business is seen as the unwind of the discount rate on in-force business.

**Economic experience variances**
- This is the impact of the differences between actual and expected investment experience over the year on the surplus emerging over the year
- and on the value of the in-force business at the end of the year. 

**Economic assumption changes**
- This is the impact of changes to the economic assumptions in the valuation basis and the embedded value projection basis.
- Economic assumption changes include the impact of changes in the discount rate.
- The resulting unexplained component of the analysis may be due to the investment return component.

<u><b>Q2(ii): Suggest reasons why the company does not publicly disclose a split by asset type.</b></u>
**Regulator** 
- It may not be a regulatory / statutory requirement.

**Competitors** 
- Competitors might not be currently publishing this information.
- Disclosing such detail may result in competitors acquiring sensitive information.
- Competitors could use the information to change their own investment strategy, aiming to out-perform this company and therefore potentially under-price them.

**Practicalities**
- The level of granularity required may not be practically achievable;
- due to not being able to access relevant data;
- or being able to do so in a timely manner.
- The company might not have sufficient resources to be able to split by asset type;
- e.g. due to its modelling capabilities.
- Splitting of assets by asset type is too complex for some asset types;
- e.g. for collective investment schemes;
- or some derivatives.
- Assigning actual assets to liabilities may be impractical;
- for some complex products.
- For some products, splitting by investment type may be misleading;
- e.g. for products with investment guarantees;
- the steps of the analysis may not be independent;
- and so when carrying out the analysis of MCEV, the results will depend on the order of the analysis.
- As the analysis is on a market-consistent basis, the expected return is based on a risk-free rate, which may be derived on an aggregate basis;
- and not split according to the asset-type.
- If tax is calculated on an aggregate basis, it may be difficult to assign to investment return per asset type;
- or too much work might be required.
- The company might use alternative metrics to measure experience internally;
- and MCEV might not be a key focus for it.
- There may currently be a large unexplained;
- and so the company lacks sufficient confidence in the MCEV results to justify the work.
- The complexity of the PVIF component may also cause limitations of the analysis.

<u><b>Q2(iii): Discuss the actions the company may take as a result of this positive investment variance.</b></u>
- Initially the company may want to carry out more detailed analysis to assist in finding out the true reason.
- In particular, it would want to check whether there is an error 
- especially if there is a large unexplained. 
- The company may decide to do nothing
- if the variance is within the company’s tolerance range
- or if it is a one-off.
- It may expect positive investment variance under MCEV.
- The company might want to amend its investment strategy to take on less investment risk.
- Material investment variance can be an indication that there is some sort of mismatching, so it may want to match its assets and liabilities more closely by duration.
- It would want to reduce its investment in risky volatile assets
- e.g. investments in equity release / commercial mortgages 
- and increase its investment in safer assets such as government bonds.
- This would reduce the likelihood of gaining a significant positive investment variance in future, but also the likelihood of a loss (negative variance).
- The company may decide to reduce the credit risk by investing in higher-rated bonds. 
- It could also reduce its credit risk by diversification
- within asset class 
- or by matching with some credit derivatives.
- It could possibly add / adjust the illiquidity premium applied to the expected return.
- The company would want to consider its future matching policy for new business.
- The company may consult the Chief Actuary before it decides to take any permanent action.

<u><b>Q2(iv): Discuss the implications, both internal and external, of the company's proposal.</b></u>
**Internal** 
- Longer-term costs reduced;
- increasing overall profits.
- Could use Solvency II own funds instead.
- By no longer producing an MCEV, resources are freed up;
- so the company can focus its attention on core activities.
- There are likely to be costs involved in making this change initially.
- Internal accounting may use analysis of change in MCEV.
- Any internal reconciliations may also use analysis of change in MCEV. 
- Internal reinsurance may use the company’s MCEV profit in profit-sharing calculations.
- MCEV profit might be used throughout the company in its management information and other metrics might not be accurate enough for internal use.

**External** 
- MCEV is not a statutory requirement so the company is within its rights to make this decision.
- Shareholders’ reactions will be important;
- as MCEV is used to show the true value of the company. 
- It will depend on what the rest of the market is doing.
- If most companies have moved away from publishing an MCEV;
- then it will be easier to present to shareholders.
- However, shareholders may not support this move;
- which could make the company vulnerable to a hostile takeover.
- As the company already produces Solvency II regulatory reports;
- which is on a realistic / best estimate basis;
- shareholders may see MCEV as excessive and favour the change.
- However there are important differences, including different terminology,
- and format of information;
- and calculations themselves, e.g. Solvency II’s matching adjustment / treatment of the illiquidity premium;
- and the approach to contract boundaries.
- Therefore any changes will need to be carefully communicated to shareholders and share analysts.
- The company should consider the reactions of credit rating agencies and distributors.
